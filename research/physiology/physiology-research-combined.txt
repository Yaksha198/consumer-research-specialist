## Issues that affect several files

- **The inputs contradict each other.** BMI 35 → 33 at a typical height is about 6 kg of loss, not the reported 9 kg. Topics 1, 5 and 6 compute both cases. Resolve this with actual height and weigh-ins before relying on any per-kg estimate.
- **The protein threshold in the brief doesn't match the subject.** 150–180 g/day at ~105–110 kg is under 2 g/kg of actual body weight (topic 4).
- **Ketosis can't be ruled out.** At 30–60 g carbs/day it confounds both the appetite question (topic 4) and the early "lean loss" readings (topic 5).
- **Most trial populations don't match him.** They are mostly lean young men (topics 1–3), or adults around 65–70 on higher-carb diets (topic 7).

## Combined estimate: a 1–1.5 kg overnight jump after a carb and salt refeed

Glycogen water 0.4–1.0 kg (topic 1), sodium water 0.2–0.6 kg (topic 2), gut contents 0.1–0.4 kg (topic 3). These overlap: insulin drives both glycogen storage and sodium retention. A 1–1.5 kg overnight jump is therefore fully explained without any fat gain [likely]. Fat gain can't physically be that fast: 1 kg of fat is about 7,000+ kcal above maintenance.

# Topic 1: Glycogen-water binding ratio

> **Verification note (read first):** In this research session the network proxy blocked every direct page fetch (PubMed, PMC, Springer, Wiley, AJCN, MDPI, JCI, Europe PMC, Crossref all returned EGRESS_BLOCKED). Each citation below was checked against the search engine's indexed copy of the PubMed or journal record, which included the title, authors, journal, volume/pages or DOI, and abstract text. Nothing here is based on a full-text read. Items tagged **(abstract only, via index)** were confirmed that way. Items tagged **UNVERIFIED** did not show up in any indexed record. Please re-fetch the PubMed pages before publishing.

## Plain-language answer
The coach's "~3 g water per g glycogen" is about right as a working number, and it is better described as **3 to 4 g water per g glycogen** [likely]. The human data behind it are thin, all come from lean and mostly trained men, and all are measured indirectly. The ratio is also not a fixed chemical constant. When extra water is available, far more than 3 g may be stored alongside each gram of glycogen, and most of the "glycogen water" is ordinary cell water that behaves osmotically like any other [likely]. Counting the glycogen itself, **each 100 g of glycogen gained or lost moves body weight by about 0.4–0.5 kg** [likely]. For this subject, who eats 30–60 g of carbohydrate a day, is insulin-resistant and does no training, one higher-carb day (150–300 g carbohydrate) probably stores about 100–250 g of glycogen. That is roughly **0.4–1.2 kg** of scale weight [guessing]. It can plausibly account for part of a 1–1.5 kg overnight jump, but usually not all of it. Gut contents (the weight of the food itself) and insulin-driven renal sodium/water retention are separate and additive contributors [likely].

## Insulin resistance / population caveat
- **Every ratio study used lean, healthy, mostly trained men**, and most started glycogen-depleted by exercise. None looked at obese or insulin-resistant people, or at anyone on a chronic 30–60 g/day intake. The ratio probably holds per gram because it reflects cell water and osmotic physics, but **how many grams get stored** will differ [likely].
- **Insulin resistance slows muscle glycogen storage.** Under matched hyperglycaemic-hyperinsulinaemic clamp conditions, people with type 2 diabetes synthesised muscle glycogen at 78±28 vs 183±39 µmol/kg muscle/min, about 43% of the normal rate (Shulman 1990). The subject is insulin-resistant, not diabetic, so his impairment is probably milder. Either way, a single refeed will likely store **less** glycogen than the lean-athlete studies suggest [likely].
- **No exercise means no exercise-driven boost to glycogen synthesis.** Every loading study used depletion exercise first. Sedentary refeeding is slower [likely].
- **Chronic low carb:** keto-adapted elite endurance athletes (Volek 2016) had resting muscle glycogen similar to high-carb athletes. This cannot be carried over to a sedentary, insulin-resistant man, whose muscle glycogen is not being drawn down by training and who has no training stimulus to rebuild it. For him the liver is the clearer effect: it drops to about 10–25% of normal within 24 h on a carb-poor diet and stays there (Nilsson & Hultman 1973) [likely].
- Liver and muscle mass are larger in obesity, so absolute stores may be larger than in lean people, not smaller [guessing].

## Sources
### 1. Olsson & Saltin 1970
- **Citation:** Olsson KE, Saltin B. 1970. Variation in total body water with muscle glycogen changes in man. *Acta Physiol Scand* 80(1):11–18. DOI 10.1111/j.1748-1716.1970.tb04764.x; PMID 5475323. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5475323/ (abstract only, via index)
- **Population:** 19 subjects (sex and body composition not stated in the abstract; the era suggests healthy young men), depleted by heavy arm and leg exercise and then 3 days of protein/fat diet.  **Mismatch vs subject:** lean, exercise-depleted, no insulin resistance.
- **Finding / number:** Carb loading for 4 days raised thigh glycogen from 4.5 to 19.9 g/kg and arm glycogen from 2.6 to 16.9 g/kg. Body weight rose 2.4 kg and total body water rose 2.2 L (tritium dilution), which the authors attributed to glycogen storage. Later reviews read this as 3–4 g water per g glycogen. **The frequently quoted "2.7 g" does not appear in the abstract.** Its origin is unclear and is UNVERIFIED as an Olsson & Saltin number.
- **Quality / limitations:** The ratio is indirect: whole-body water is divided by glycogen extrapolated from biopsies, and liver glycogen was not measured.

### 2. Fernández-Elías et al. 2015
- **Citation:** Fernández-Elías VE, Ortega JF, Nelson RK, Mora-Rodriguez R. 2015. Relationship between muscle water and glycogen recovery after prolonged exercise in the heat in humans. *Eur J Appl Physiol* 115:1919–1926. DOI 10.1007/s00421-015-3175-z; PMID 25911631. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25911631/ (abstract only, via index)
- **Population:** 9 aerobically trained subjects, dehydrated by 4.6% during 150 min of cycling in the heat.  **Mismatch vs subject:** trained, lean, dehydrated and depleted, acute setting.
- **Finding / number:** Recovery with 250 g carbohydrate in 400 mL water stored about 3 g water per g glycogen (1:3). The same carbohydrate plus about 3.17 L water gave about 1:17. The authors conclude "at least 3 g" per g glycogen, and attribute the extra water to storage not bound to glycogen.
- **Quality / limitations:** Small sample, and this is the only direct biopsy test of the ratio. It shows the ratio depends on how much water is taken in.

### 3. Kreitzman, Coxon & Szaz 1992
- **Citation:** Kreitzman SN, Coxon AY, Szaz KF. 1992. Glycogen storage: illusions of easy weight loss, excessive weight regain, and distortions in estimates of body composition. *Am J Clin Nutr* 56(1 Suppl):292S–293S. PMID 1615908. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1615908/ (abstract only, via index; DOI not seen)
- **Population:** 11 dieters on a VLCD for 4 days (presumably obese).  **Mismatch vs subject:** VLCD rather than low carb at about 2000 kcal. Closer in adiposity to the subject.
- **Finding / number:** Glycogen is stored in liver, muscle and fat cells with 3–4 parts water and 0.45 mmol K/g. Early changes in total body potassium mainly reflect glycogen. The paper warns about "exaggerated regain" when carbohydrate is loaded again. The abstract gives no numeric glycogen mass for the 11 subjects.
- **Quality / limitations:** A two-page conference supplement. The 3–4 figure is asserted rather than newly measured.

### 4. Nilsson & Hultman 1973 (liver)
- **Citation:** Nilsson LH, Hultman E. 1973. Liver glycogen in man — the effect of total starvation or a carbohydrate-poor diet followed by carbohydrate refeeding. *Scand J Clin Lab Invest* 32(4):325–330. DOI 10.3109/00365517309084355 (abstract only, via index; PMID not seen)
- **Population:** 19 human subjects, repeated percutaneous liver biopsies.  **Mismatch vs subject:** healthy, and adiposity is unknown.
- **Finding / number:** Liver glycogen was 232 mmol/kg after an overnight fast. It fell to 24–55 within 24 h of starvation or a carb-poor diet, stayed low for up to 9 more days, and rebounded to 424–624 mmol/kg on refeeding.
- **Quality / limitations:** Old, invasive and small, but still the standing human biopsy reference.

### Supporting / conflicting sources
- **Yang & Van Itallie 1976**, *J Clin Invest* 58(3):722–730, https://www.jci.org/articles/view/108519 (abstract only, via index; DOI/PMID not seen). 6 obese subjects in 10-day periods. The 800-kcal ketogenic diet lost 466.6 g/day, of which 61.2% was water. The isocaloric mixed diet lost 277.9 g/day, 37.1% water. **This is the closest population match.**
- **King, Jones & O'Hara 2018**, *Eur J Appl Physiol*, "The availability of water associated with glycogen during dehydration: a reservoir or raindrop?" DOI 10.1007/s00421-017-3768-9; PMID 29196846 (abstract only, via index; volume/pages UNVERIFIED). At about 2% glycogen concentration, glycogen-associated water is fully osmotically available. At 5–10%, only about 10–20% of it is "protected". It is not a separate reservoir. (This is probably the "King et al. 2018" the coach half-remembered. It is in EJAP, not J Appl Physiol.)
- **Sherman et al. 1982**, *Int J Sports Med* 3:22–24, DOI 10.1055/s-2008-1026056, PMID 7068293. **Conflicting.** In rats, muscle glycogen ranged from 10.0 to 30.2 µmol/g, and muscle water "showed no consistent relationship" to glycogen. This is rodent data.
- **Shiose et al. 2016**, *J Appl Physiol* 121:205–211, DOI 10.1152/japplphysiol.00126.2016. n=8, 12 g/kg/day carbohydrate for 72 h, 13C-MRS glycogen and deuterium total body water. Total body water rose, mainly as intracellular water. The introduction cites 2.7–4 g/g. Numeric results were not seen.
- **Bone et al. 2017**, *Med Sci Sports Exerc*, DOI 10.1249/MSS.0000000000001174, PMID 28410328. Glycogen loading in 18 trained cyclists raised total body water by 2.3% and DXA "lean mass" by 2–3%. Glycogen swings therefore distort body-composition scans.
- **Shulman et al. 1990**, *NEJM* 322:223–228, DOI 10.1056/NEJM199001253220403, PMID 2403659. Numbers are under the caveat above.
- **Acheson et al. 1988**, *Am J Clin Nutr*, PMID 3165600 (volume/pages UNVERIFIED). n=3 men. Glycogen capacity is about 15 g/kg body weight, and roughly 500 g can be gained before net lipogenesis begins.
- **Murray & Rosenbloom 2018**, *Nutr Rev* 76(4):243–259, DOI 10.1093/nutrit/nuy001. Whole-body glycogen is about 600 g (the figure was seen only in an index summary).
- **Volek et al. 2016**, *Metabolism*, PMID 26892521 (volume/pages UNVERIFIED). n=20 elite ultra-endurance athletes on about 20 months of low carb. Resting muscle glycogen was not different from high-carb athletes.

## Disagreements between sources
- **The ratio itself:** Olsson & Saltin and Kreitzman give 3–4. Fernández-Elías found "at least 3" and up to 17 with free water. Sherman found no consistent relationship in rats. King argues most of the water is not specifically "bound". **Resolution:** 3–4 g/g is a reasonable minimum for scale-weight estimates. The upper end is set by hydration and sodium, not by glycogen chemistry.
- **The "2.7 g" figure** is widely repeated. It does not appear in the Olsson & Saltin abstract and was seen only as part of a "2.7–4" range in Shiose 2016. Its original source is unconfirmed.
- **Low-carb baseline:** Volek (athletes) found normal muscle glycogen on keto. Nilsson & Hultman found the liver collapses on a carb-poor diet. These are compatible, because muscle and liver respond differently. For a sedentary person it is unknown whether muscle glycogen stays normal.

## Applying it to the subject
Assumptions: about 100–110 kg, skeletal muscle about 35 kg [guessing], liver about 1.8 kg [guessing].

1. **Mixed-diet stores:** muscle about 35 kg × 13–16 g/kg ≈ 450–560 g, plus liver about 1.8 kg × ~38 g/kg ≈ 70 g. Total ≈ **520–630 g** [likely].
2. **Chronic 30–60 g/day stores:** liver ≈ 7–16 g (4–9 g/kg). Muscle is uncertain: perhaps 250–450 g [guessing]. The total is about **260–470 g**, roughly 150–350 g below mixed-diet levels.
3. **Weight effect of the initial switch to low carb:** 150–350 g glycogen × 4–5 (1 g glycogen plus 3–4 g water) ≈ **0.6–1.75 kg**. Add the natriuresis of carb restriction (see sodium topic). Yang & Van Itallie's 61% water share is consistent with this. Of a reported 9 kg loss in 5 weeks, perhaps 1–2.5 kg is glycogen-linked water plus sodium-linked water [likely]. (The BMI change 35 to 33 implies only about 6 kg at typical height, so this discrepancy should be checked. If the true loss is about 6 kg, the water share is proportionally larger.)
4. **One higher-carb day, worked estimate** (ratio 3–4 g/g, glycogen mass included):
   - +150 g glycogen: 150 × 4 to 150 × 5 = **0.60–0.75 kg**
   - +300 g glycogen: 300 × 4 to 300 × 5 = **1.2–1.5 kg**
   - Storing 300 g of glycogen in one day, with insulin resistance and no exercise, probably needs well over 400 g of carbohydrate. A 150–300 g carbohydrate day more plausibly stores about 100–200 g, which is **0.4–1.0 kg** [guessing].
5. **Maximum plausible swing** from low-carb baseline to fully loaded (about 800–1,000 g), counting only glycogen and its water: ≈ (400–700 g) × 4–5 ≈ **1.6–3.5 kg**. Olsson & Saltin measured 2.4 kg empirically [likely].
6. **Confound:** refeeding raises insulin, which increases renal sodium reabsorption and so retains extracellular water. This is a separate mechanism from glycogen-bound (intracellular) water, and it is *not* included in the 3–4 g/g ratio. Glycogen storage also takes up potassium (0.45 mmol/g). Undigested food and fibre in the gut add their own mass. A 1–1.5 kg overnight gain after a refeed is therefore likely a mix of about 0.4–1.0 kg glycogen water, plus sodium-retained water, plus gut contents. It should not be read as fat gain [likely].

Confidence: medium. The 3–4 g/g ratio agrees across sources and matches the whole-body data, but every ratio study is in lean, trained, exercise-depleted men. The subject's actual stores and his storage rate under insulin resistance are estimated, not measured. And no source could be fetched in full for this write-up.

# Topic 2: Sodium and water retention kinetics

> **Verification note (read first):** During this research session the network egress proxy blocked every page fetch (PubMed, PMC, Europe PMC, journal sites, Crossref). Citations below were checked against the **search-engine index of the PubMed or journal page**: title, authors, journal, volume and pages, plus the abstract text shown in the search result. None were read in full. Each one is marked "(abstract/index only)". The worked arithmetic is my own calculation from those numbers.

## Plain-language answer
Extra sodium is held with water: roughly 1 L (≈1 kg) of water for every ~3.2 g of sodium kept in the extracellular fluid. The kidneys clear a salt surplus in a roughly exponential way, with a half-time of about 21 h, and reach a new balance in about 3 days. So if you ate ~2.3 g more sodium (≈1 tsp salt) than usual yesterday, the scale this morning might show about **+0.2 to +0.6 kg**. Most of that is gone within **2-3 days** once intake returns to normal [likely]. That is far too fast and too large to be fat: 0.5 kg of fat overnight would need a surplus of several thousand kcal [certain]. Two things can make the salt effect bigger or smaller. First, long-term Mars-simulation studies show the body can gain or lose several hundred mmol of sodium with no matching change in weight, so the "sodium always carries water" rule does not hold exactly [likely]. Second, for a low-carb dieter, a meal that is both salty and high in carbs makes things worse. Insulin roughly halves how fast the kidney excretes sodium, and bringing back carbs after restriction causes fast sodium and water retention [likely].

## The numbers
| Quantity | Value | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Sodium in 1 tsp salt (5.8 g NaCl) | ~2.3 g = ~100 mmol | 2300 mg ÷ 23 mg/mmol [certain] |
| Isotonic equivalence (ECF Na ~140 mmol/L) | 1 L water per 140 mmol = 3.2 g Na = 8.2 g NaCl; ≈7 g water per mmol Na; ≈310 g water per g Na | arithmetic [certain as a ceiling if retained isotonically] |
| 100 mmol retained isotonically | ≈0.71 L ≈ 0.71 kg | arithmetic |
| Half-time of transition between sodium steady states | ~21 h (Bie review); 21.8 ± 2.4 h during restriction (Sagnella) | Sources 1, 2 |
| ΔTotal-body Na / Δ(daily Na intake) | ≈1.3 days (i.e., +100 mmol/day sustained → ≈+130 mmol TBS ≈ 0.9 L if isotonic) | Source 1 |
| Time to new steady state after raising intake | within ~3 days (not mono-exponential) | Source 2 |
| Non-water-linked swings in total-body Na at fixed intake | ±200-400 mmol, with no parallel change in body weight or ECW | Source 3 |
| Insulin effect on urinary Na excretion (clamp) | 401 → 213 µeq/min (−47%), GFR unchanged | Source 4 |
| Same in obese vs lean adolescents | −54% obese vs −51% non-obese (effect preserved despite insulin resistance) | Supporting (Rocchini) |
| 3-day fast | ~800 g/day weight loss with progressive natriuresis; carbohydrate refeeding → rapid Na retention | Supporting (Veverbrants & Arky) |

## Insulin resistance / population caveat
- **The sodium-retaining effect of insulin is kept in insulin resistance.** Rocchini et al. 1989 found that obese adolescents, who were much less sensitive to insulin for glucose uptake, reduced their sodium excretion by the same amount as lean controls. This is the "selective insulin resistance" idea: the body resists insulin's effect on glucose but not its effect on sodium. For a person with HOMA-IR 3.58, a carb meal and its bigger insulin spike can probably cause *more* sodium retention than in a lean person, not less [likely].
- **The low-carb state removes this brake.** Low insulin plus ketosis causes natriuresis (extra sodium loss in urine). On an 800 kcal ketogenic diet, 61% of the weight lost by obese subjects over 10 days was water, against 37% on a mixed diet (Yang & Van Itallie 1976, JCI 58:722-30, doi:10.1172/JCI108519; abstract/index only). Bringing back carbs reverses this quickly (Veverbrants & Arky). So a salty, carb-heavy meal for this subject sets off **three** water-gaining effects at once: the sodium load itself, insulin-driven retention of sodium already in the body, and water stored with glycogen (glycogen is covered in another topic, not verified here). The total can easily be above the salt-only estimate [likely].
- **Obesity and diabetes may change tissue sodium storage.** 23Na-MRI found higher muscle sodium (20.6 vs 16.3 mmol/L) and skin sodium (24.5 vs 20.6 mmol/L) in type 2 diabetes (n=59) than in people with primary hypertension (Kannenkeril et al. 2019, J Diabetes Complications 33(7):485-489, doi:10.1016/j.jdiacomp.2019.04.006, PMID 31101486; abstract/index only). Whether this shifts short-term water kinetics in someone who is insulin-resistant but not diabetic is **unknown** [guessing].
- **All the core kinetic data come from a different population.** The subjects were healthy, mostly normal-weight young men on fixed metabolic-ward diets. None were obese, insulin-resistant or on low-carb diets.
- **Discrepancy in the reported weight loss.** The subject reports ~9 kg lost, but a BMI change from 35 to 33 suggests ~6 kg. Early low-carb loss is largely water (see above). That could explain the gap, and it also means several kg could come back quickly as water after a carb and salt refeed, without any fat gain.

## Sources
### 1. Bie 2018 review (standing quantitative model)
- **Citation:** Bie P. 2018. Mechanisms of sodium balance: total body sodium, surrogate variables, and renal sodium excretion. *Am J Physiol Regul Integr Comp Physiol*. doi:10.1152/ajpregu.00363.2017. https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/ajpregu.00363.2017 (abstract/index only; volume and pages not confirmed)
- **Population:** Review of human balance studies. **Mismatch vs subject:** mostly healthy normal-weight adults.
- **Finding / number:** Total-body Na ≈4,200 mmol. Transitions between steady states have T½ ≈21 h. ΔTBS/Δ(intake/day) ≈1.3 days. The review also covers "uneven, nonosmotic distribution" of extra sodium, mainly in skin, and long-term instability of total-body sodium even when intake is constant.
- **Quality / limitations:** Authoritative synthesis. It openly says the classic model is incomplete.

### 2. Sagnella et al. 1990 (direct kinetic measurement)
- **Citation:** Sagnella GA, Markandu ND, Singer DR, MacGregor GA. 1990. Kinetics of renal sodium excretion during changes in dietary sodium intake in man: an exponential process? *Clin Exp Hypertens A* 12(2):171-178. doi:10.3109/10641969009074726. https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.3109/10641969009074726 (abstract/index only; PMID not confirmed)
- **Population:** Normal subjects. n=8 went from normal intake to 10 mmol/day for 6 days; n=6 went from 10 to 350 mmol/day for 5 days. **Mismatch vs subject:** healthy, not obese, large step changes.
- **Finding / number:** When intake was cut, excretion fell mono-exponentially with T½ 21.8 ± 2.4 h. When intake was raised, excretion reached the new steady state **within 3 days** but did not follow a single exponential.
- **Quality / limitations:** Small n. Weight and body water were not reported in the abstract.

### 3. Rakova et al. 2013 and Lerchl et al. 2015 (Mars-500 ultra-long balance)
- **Citation:** Rakova N, Jüttner K, Dahlmann A, et al. 2013. Long-term space flight simulation reveals infradian rhythmicity in human Na+ balance. *Cell Metab* 17(1):125-131. PMID 23312287. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23312287/. Companion paper: Lerchl K, Rakova N, Dahlmann A, et al. 2015. Agreement between 24-hour salt ingestion and sodium excretion in a controlled environment. *Hypertension* (Oct 2015). PMID 26259596, doi:10.1161/HYPERTENSIONAHA.115.05851 (both abstract/index only)
- **Population:** 10 healthy men in 105-day (n=4) and 205-day (n=6) confinement, with salt fixed at 12, 9 and 6 g/day. **Mismatch vs subject:** healthy, sedentary confinement, not obese.
- **Finding / number:** Urinary sodium excretion followed weekly rhythms driven by aldosterone. Total-body Na moved by **±200-400 mmol** over roughly monthly cycles **without parallel changes in body weight or extracellular water**. Across the whole study, 92% of dietary salt was recovered in urine. A single 24-h urine could not detect a 3 g/day difference in salt intake.
- **Quality / limitations:** Unusually precise long-term control, but very small n.

### 4. DeFronzo et al. 1975 (insulin antinatriuresis)
- **Citation:** DeFronzo RA, Cooke CR, Andres R, Faloona GR, Davis PJ. 1975. The effect of insulin on renal handling of sodium, potassium, calcium, and phosphate in man. *J Clin Invest* 55:845-855. doi:10.1172/JCI107996. PMID 1120786. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1120786/ (abstract/index only)
- **Population:** 6 water-loaded normal subjects on a euglycaemic insulin infusion (2 mU/kg/min; insulin 98-193 µU/mL). **Mismatch vs subject:** lean, healthy, insulin levels above typical post-meal values.
- **Finding / number:** Urinary sodium excretion fell from 401 ± 46 to 213 ± 18 µeq/min with no change in GFR or renal plasma flow. The site was the distal nephron.
- **Quality / limitations:** Acute, n=6, infusion model rather than a meal.

**Supporting / conflicting sources (abstract/index only):**
- Heer M, Baisch F, Kropp J, Gerzer R, Drummer C. 2000. *Am J Physiol Renal* 278(4):F585-F595, doi:10.1152/ajprenal.2000.278.4.F585, PMID 10751219. 32 healthy men, NaCl 50/200/400/550 mmol/day. Sodium balance was positive and plasma volume rose (+315 ± 37 mL at 550 mmol), but **total body water and body mass did not increase**. A later criticism is that potassium balance was not accounted for.
- Rakova N, et al. 2017. *J Clin Invest* 127(5):1932-1943, PMID 28414302. At +6 g/day salt, men drank *less* water and conserved water through urine concentration.
- Rocchini AP, Katch V, et al. 1989. *Hypertension* 14:367-374, doi:10.1161/01.HYP.14.4.367. Insulin cut sodium excretion by 54% in obese adolescents and 51% in non-obese young adults.
- Veverbrants E, Arky RA. 1969. *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 29(1):55-62, doi:10.1210/jcem-29-1-55. Carbohydrate refeeding after a 3-day fast caused rapid sodium retention; fat refeeding did not.
- Brands MW, Manhiani MM. 2012. *Am J Physiol Regul Integr Comp Physiol* 303:R1101-R1109, doi:10.1152/ajpregu.00390.2012. Insulin's *chronic* sodium-retaining and hypertensive role is contested (insulinoma patients are not hypertensive; dog studies were negative).
- Strauss MB, et al. 1958. *AMA Arch Intern Med* 102:527-536. The original "kinetic concept": total-body sodium keeps fluctuating around a set point.

## Disagreements between sources
- **Isotonic model vs nonosmotic storage.** The classic balance model (Bie's description of the classic model, Sagnella, Strauss) assumes retained sodium brings isotonic water with it. Heer 2000 and Rakova 2013 found positive sodium balance, or total-body Na swings of 200-400 mmol (isotonic equivalent ≈1.4-2.9 L), **without** matching weight change. The critics' reply, from dog balance studies and the potassium-accounting critique of Heer, is that part of this is measurement error. This is unresolved. In practice it means the isotonic figure is an **upper bound** for water retention, not a fixed constant.
- **Speed of the rise vs the fall.** Excretion falls mono-exponentially (T½ ≈21 h) when sodium is cut. When sodium is added it adapts within 3 days but not mono-exponentially (Sagnella). So a single-exponential washout model is only approximate.
- **Acute vs chronic insulin effect.** The acute antinatriuresis is well replicated (DeFronzo, Rocchini). Whether it causes lasting sodium retention is disputed (Brands & Manhiani). For a question about the next day's scale reading, the acute effect is the one that matters.

## Applying it to the subject
**Scenario:** baseline 2-3 g Na/day (~90-130 mmol), plus 2.3 g (100 mmol) on one day, then back to baseline.
- **Isotonic ceiling:** 100 mmol ÷ 140 mmol/L ≈ 0.7 kg if none of it had been excreted.
- **Next morning** (~12-18 h after the average time of intake), using τ ≈ 1.3 d (T½ ≈ 21 h): retained fraction ≈ e^(−12/31) to e^(−18/31), or 0.56-0.68. That leaves 56-68 mmol, **≈0.4-0.5 kg** if isotonic.
- **Adjusting for nonosmotic storage and day-to-day noise** (Heer, Rakova): realistic range **+0.2 to +0.6 kg** [likely]. Salt alone would rarely push this above ~0.7 kg [likely].
- **Washout:** about 0.2 kg left on day 2, under 0.1 kg by day 3, and effectively gone by 72-96 h [likely]. That assumes water and potassium intake stay normal.
- **If the salty meal also contained substantial carbohydrate** after weeks at 30-60 g/day: add insulin-driven retention of sodium already in the body and glycogen water. **+0.5 to 1.5 kg next morning** is plausible, returning to trend over 2-4 days of going back to low carb [guessing: not directly measured in this population].
- **Practical rule:** a jump overnight or across 1-2 days that fades within 3-4 days is water. Fat gain shows up as a slow trend that lasts beyond a week. Judge progress on a 7-day rolling average, and treat the morning after a restaurant meal or a carb refeed as noise. A true 0.5 kg fat gain would need an energy surplus of several thousand kcal (standard ~7,000-7,700 kcal/kg adipose tissue approximation, not re-verified here).

Confidence: medium, because the isotonic arithmetic and the ~21 h half-time are solid, but the core data come from small groups of healthy lean men, the Titze/Heer work shows the water-per-sodium link is weaker than the classic model assumes, no study measured next-morning weight after a single mixed salt-and-carb meal in obese insulin-resistant people, and I could only check abstracts and index records (no full text).

# Topic 3: Meal / gut-content weight

## Plain-language answer
Any food you have eaten but not yet passed adds to what the scale shows. How much depends on where the food is. A standard small test meal mostly leaves the stomach within about 4 hours, and fluids are absorbed fast, so by the next morning very little of last night's dinner is still in the stomach. What remains is the colon's standing content. MRI puts it at about 0.5-0.6 kg in healthy adults (roughly 480 mL on a low-fibre diet and 620 mL on a high-fibre diet). About a third of that turns over each day through meals and bowel movements. Adults in Western countries pass roughly 100-130 g of stool a day on average. So the day-to-day scale swing that comes purely from "food in transit" is on the order of **0.1-0.4 kg**. It can reach about 0.5 kg or more after a large late meal, a skipped bowel movement, or both. That is real, but it is smaller than the swings from water and glycogen. A low-carb, low-fibre diet lowers stool mass and the standing colonic content. It also makes constipation more common, which makes the day-to-day number noisier.

# Topic 4: Protein leverage and appetite suppression — does 2-3 days of high protein cause a low-intake day afterwards?

> **Verification note (read first):** This session's network policy blocked every literature page (PubMed, PMC, Europe PMC, Wiley, PLOS, Crossref and others all returned EGRESS_BLOCKED). Each citation below was checked against search-engine result metadata: the title, journal, volume/pages, and the PMID or DOI shown in the indexed PubMed/journal URL, plus the abstract-level summary. No full text was read. Every number here should be read as **(abstract only, via search index)**. Anything I could not confirm even that far is marked **UNVERIFIED**.

## Plain-language answer
Three separate things are being run together here. (1) **Acute protein satiety** is real and well replicated. At ~25-30% of energy, protein reduces ad libitum intake on the same day and over weeks, and Weigle 2005 showed this with carbohydrate held at 50%, so no ketosis was involved [certain]. (2) **The protein leverage hypothesis (PLH)** says something else. Humans regulate absolute protein intake more tightly than energy, so a *low protein proportion* makes people overeat total energy. Its best-supported arm is the low-protein side [likely]. Whether intake falls *below* energy needs once the protein target is exceeded is contested: Gosby 2011 found no drop going from 15% to 25% protein, while Martens 2013/2014 found drops at 30% [likely contested]. (3) **A lagged, discrete "low day" after 2-3 high-protein days** is not a named or documented phenomenon. I found no study showing multi-day carryover of this kind. The main candidate mechanism, FGF21, is a signal of protein *deficit*, not protein excess [likely]. The subject also eats low-carb (~30-60 g/day), so mild ketosis is a live confound. Normal day-to-day intake varies with a CV of ~23-25%, and busy 12-hour workdays and missed meals add to that. So the coach's pattern is most likely **overfitting** [likely]. The protein is probably lowering his average intake, but it probably does not explain the particular low days.

## The numbers
| Quantity | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Ad lib intake reduction, 30% vs 15% protein (carbs fixed 50%) | −441 ± 63 kcal/d; −4.9 kg over 12 wk | Weigle 2005 |
| Energy intake, 10% vs 15% protein (4-day periods) | +12% on 10%; 15%→25% produced **no change** | Gosby 2011 |
| Energy intake, 5/15/30% protein, 12 days (milk/plant) | 9.33 / 9.62 / **7.21** MJ/d | Martens 2013 |
| Energy intake, 5/15/30% protein, 12 days (beef) | 9.48 / 9.30 / **8.73** MJ/d | Martens 2014 |
| Meta-analysis, 38 ad lib trials, protein 8-54% energy | Consistent inverse relation of % protein to energy intake | Gosby 2014 |
| HP ketogenic (4% CHO) vs HP moderate-CHO (35%), obese men | 7.25 vs 7.95 MJ/d (~−170 kcal/d, the ketogenic effect on top of protein) | Johnstone 2008 |
| Protein dose reviewers associate with appetite benefit | 1.2-1.6 g/kg/d; ~25-30 g/meal | Leidy 2015 |
| Within-person day-to-day CV of energy intake | mean ~23% (range 15-45%); ~25% (16-34%) | Tarasuk & Beaton 1991; Dietitian II 2013 |
| Next-day negative autocorrelation of intake | Not found (positive lag-1 correlation); earlier negative findings attributed to a normalization artifact | Levitsky 2017 |

## Insulin resistance / population caveat
- **Almost every PLH trial used lean or normal-weight people.** Gosby 2011 (n=22) was lean. Martens 2013 had a mean BMI of 23.7, Martens 2014 24.4, and Weigle 2005 used healthy non-obese adults. The only obese-male trial here, Johnstone 2008, is a ketogenic comparison. None studied insulin-resistant men at a BMI of ~33-35.
- **Most "high protein" arms kept carbohydrate moderate.** The subject's diet combines high protein with low carbohydrate, and it is closest to Johnstone 2008's ketogenic arm. There, cutting carbohydrate added a further ~0.7 MJ/d (~170 kcal) reduction beyond protein alone. On ~30-60 g carbohydrate/day, mild ketosis is plausible, particularly on low-intake days. **Ketosis therefore cannot be excluded as a confound.** Gibson 2015 found ketogenic low-carb diets modestly reduce hunger and desire to eat.
- **The subject's per-kg intake is below the question's threshold.** Take ~107 kg (BMI 35 at an assumed 1.75 m). Then 150-180 g/day is **~1.4-1.7 g/kg of actual weight**, not >2 g/kg. It would exceed 2 g/kg only per kg of lean or ideal weight. As a share of energy it is high: ~600-720 kcal of ~2000-2100, or **~29-35% of energy**, which is the range where Martens saw reduced intake.
- The FGF21 human data (Nicolaisen 2025) come from **lean** men on protein *restriction*. They say nothing direct about high intake in obesity.

Protocols differed (4 vs 12 days, menu design, protein source), and none of the trials was in obese or insulin-resistant people.
- **Whether intake compensates from day to day.** de Castro's classic work describes 2-3-day lagged compensation. Secondary sources say other groups placed the lag at 3-5 days. Levitsky 2017 found no negative autocorrelation at all and attributes the earlier findings to a statistical artifact. I could not fetch de Castro's papers, so **the specific lags are UNVERIFIED**.
- **How much of the high-protein effect is ketosis.** Weigle shows protein alone is enough. Johnstone 2008 and Veldhorst 2010 show ketosis adds to it. The two effects are additive, and in the subject they cannot be separated.

## Applying it to the subject
- **Mean and SD of his daily intake.** At ~2050 kcal/day and a CV of ~23-25%, one within-person SD is **~470-510 kcal**. A "very low" day of ~1300-1500 kcal is only about 1-1.5 SD below his mean. Under normal variation, roughly one day in 6-7 falls more than 1 SD low, which is about once a week, before counting 12-hour workdays and skipped meals.
- **Why "after 2-3 high-protein days" is not a real test.** He eats ~150-180 g protein almost every day. So *every* low day follows 2-3 high-protein days by default. The pattern has no contrast condition, which makes it textbook selection or overfitting.
- **Which confounds are live.** Mild ketosis (carbohydrate ~30-60 g, lower still on a missed-meal day) is one. Workload and meal skipping are another. Regression to the mean after higher-intake days is a third.
- **What would test it.** Log intake daily for 4-6 weeks, including protein grams, carbohydrate grams, and a workday-intensity flag. Then check whether a low-intake day is predicted by protein from the previous 2-3 days *after* adjusting for workload. A cheap optional extra is urine ketone strips.
- **What is reasonable to believe.** His ~30-35%-of-energy protein share plus low carbohydrate is probably suppressing his *average* intake by a few hundred kcal/day. That is consistent with Weigle, Martens and Johnstone [likely]. A specific lagged "protein rebound low day" is not supported [likely].

Confidence: **medium**. The component findings are well established and consistent at abstract level: protein satiety, the PLH's proportion-based claim, and FGF21 as a low-protein signal. But no study directly tests multi-day carryover, the trial populations do not match the subject, and I could verify sources only from search-index abstracts, not full texts.

# Topic 5: Body composition partitioning during aggressive deficit, high protein, no resistance training

> **Verification note (read first):** The egress proxy blocked every literature host (PubMed, PMC, Wiley, Cambridge, AJCN, ScienceDirect, Semantic Scholar, Crossref, Europe PMC). No page could be fetched directly. Each citation below was checked against search-engine index results for the PubMed or journal page: title, journal, volume, PMID/DOI, and abstract-level numbers. They are marked **(search-index abstract; page not fetched)**. Anything I could not confirm that way is marked **UNVERIFIED**. The search budget ran out before I could confirm the remaining items.

## Plain-language answer
When overweight adults diet without resistance training, the lean ("fat-free") share of the weight they lose is not a fixed 25%. In the first weeks it is often about 50–60%, falling to about 25–35% by 6 months. What drives it: higher starting body fat lowers the lean share; men show a higher lean share than women; faster or more severe restriction raises it; a low-carb start raises it, mostly through glycogen-bound water. Higher protein (roughly >1.05–1.6 g/kg) reliably reduces lean loss by only about 0.4–0.6 kg compared with standard protein. That makes it *lean-sparing*, not *lean-building*. The well-known "lean gain in a deficit" result (Longland 2016) needed six days a week of resistance plus interval training. I found no evidence that high protein alone produces measurable lean gain during a large deficit, even in people starting above 30% body fat. A bicep circumference rise over one week with no training is most plausibly measurement error or fluid, not new muscle.

## Insulin resistance / population caveat
- **Low-carb confound:** Krieger's meta-regression and Hall's ketogenic metabolic-ward study both found *more* measured FFM loss on low-carb or ketogenic diets. Glycogen is stored with 3–4 parts water (Kreitzman 1992), and DXA and BIA count that water as "lean". So on this subject's diet (~30–60 g carb/day), early apparent lean loss is inflated by water, not muscle protein [likely]. The opposite also holds: any carb refeed will show as a "lean gain".
- **Insulin resistance itself:** I found no fetched source on whether insulin resistance changes the lean fraction during a deficit. The idea of "anabolic resistance" in obesity is plausible but **UNVERIFIED** here.
- **Protein studies are mostly short, supervised trials** in young, often normal-weight or active adults (Pasiakos, Longland) or adults over 50 (Kim). None matches a 32-year-old Class II obese, insulin-resistant man on a self-directed plan.
- **Sex:** Heymsfield's data give men a *higher* early lean fraction than women, which is unfavourable for this male subject.

# Topic 6: HOMA-IR improvement timeline with weight loss alone (no resistance training)

> **Verification note:** The egress proxy blocked direct page fetches from PubMed, PMC, journal sites and Europe PMC (403 policy denial). I checked every citation below against the text of its PubMed or journal page as returned by web search, so each is marked **(search-snippet/abstract only)**. I never read full texts. Anything I could not confirm is labelled **UNVERIFIED**.

## Plain-language answer
The fasting measures (fasting insulin, and HOMA-IR, which mostly reflects the liver) improve quickly once someone is in a calorie deficit. The liver responds within 48 hours to 7 days, before much weight is gone. After that, the improvement tracks how much weight is lost. The best per-kg figure I found is about **0.15 HOMA-IR units per kg lost** (roughly 6.5 kg per 1 point). A large 8-week trial found about **−1.4 units after ~11% weight loss**. For someone starting at 3.58, that means a fall of about 30–40% after 6–10 kg of loss, giving a true value of roughly **2.0–2.7** at 12 weeks. A very-low-carb diet probably pushes fasting insulin a bit lower than weight loss alone would. However, HOMA-IR varies about 27% within the same person from week to week. A single retest could therefore read anywhere from about 1.5 to 3.2 even if the improvement is real. Only a drop of more than about 47–52% counts as proven change from one pair of measurements.

|---|---|---|
| Weight loss needed to lower HOMA-IR by 1 point | **6.5 kg** (adjusted for baseline); 2.9 kg per 1 µU/mL fasting insulin | EJCN 2016/17, n=50 |
| HOMA-IR change after an 8-week low-energy diet with ~11% weight loss | **−1.4** (men −1.50, women −1.35) | PREVIEW, n=2,224 |
| Dose-response, measured by clamp | Liver and adipose insulin sensitivity largely improved by ~5% loss (claimed to plateau at ~5–8%); muscle keeps improving to 16% | Magkos 2016 |

## Insulin resistance / population caveat
- **Most of the rapid-reversal data come from people with type 2 diabetes** (Lim, Petersen, DiRECT, Goldenberg). Their hepatic insulin resistance and liver fat start much higher, so they improve more in absolute terms. The subject's HOMA-IR of 3.58 is insulin-resistant but his glycaemic status is unknown. Prediabetes data (PREVIEW, Cell Metab 2026) and obese non-diabetic data (Magkos, EJCN) match him better. [likely]
- **HOMA-IR mostly measures hepatic, fasting insulin resistance.** It does not capture muscle insulin sensitivity. Muscle is where the lack of resistance training matters: in Beals 2023, diet plus exercise gave about 2× the whole-body (mainly muscle) insulin-sensitivity gain of diet alone at matched 10% loss. So skipping training probably costs less on HOMA-IR than on clamp or OGTT measures. [likely]
- **Very-low-carb diets affect the two inputs in different ways.** They lower fasting insulin partly through carbohydrate restriction itself, not only weight loss (Kirk; Cell Metab 2026). They may also slightly raise fasting glucose through reduced glucose uptake in muscle, or "glucose sparing". In Bisschop 2001, a eucaloric high-fat, low-carb diet in healthy men reduced insulin's ability to suppress liver glucose output. Because HOMA-IR = glucose (mmol/L) × insulin (µU/mL) / 22.5, a lower insulin and a higher glucose can partly cancel out. The effect on glucose is supported mechanistically. I found no verified size for it in obese insulin-resistant adults. [guessing on size]
- **Active energy deficit versus weight-stable state:** Kirk showed that 48 h of restriction improves hepatic insulin sensitivity before meaningful weight loss. A retest taken during an active deficit therefore includes a "deficit effect" that may partly fade at maintenance. [likely]
- **Population mismatch:** most participants were middle-aged (44–50), and many trials were mostly women. The subject is a 32-year-old man. Lim 2011 was 9/11 male. [certain]

# Topic 7: Best movement combination for a time-constrained beginner

> **Verification note:** Direct fetches of PubMed, PMC, Wiley, Springer and journal pages were blocked by this session's network egress policy. Every number below comes from a search-engine-indexed abstract or record of the named paper (so all of it is "(abstract only)"). Items marked UNVERIFIED were not confirmed in any retrieved text.

## Plain-language answer
During a diet, resistance training (RT) is the only exercise shown to reliably protect lean mass. In dieting obese older adults, RT prevented about 0.8 kg (≈93%) of the lean mass that diet alone lost. Walking and aerobic work do not do this, and in one trial they made lean-mass loss worse. The RT dose that works is small. When weekly volume is held equal, training a muscle once, twice or three times a week gives similar growth, so a short session at home counts. Walking is the easier habit to keep. More steps are linked to lower mortality, with benefit levelling off at about 8,000–10,000 steps/day under age 60. Short walks after meals lower glucose after eating, but mostly after carb-heavy meals, and this subject eats very little carbohydrate. The adherence evidence decides the plan. About half of people drop out of structured programmes within 6 months. Lifestyle activity worked as well as a gym prescription over 24 months. Step tracking with a goal adds about 2,500 steps/day. Habits form slowly (median 66 days) and survive a missed day. **Recommendation:** in month one, make home RT the one fixed commitment, done as short daily "snacks" or 2 × ~20-min sessions. Treat walking as a step target filled in around the day, not as a scheduled session. [likely]

## Insulin resistance / population caveat
- **Lean-mass trials are in older adults.** Sardeli (six RCTs), Villareal and Beavers (mean age ~67) all studied people aged about 65–70. Those people start with less muscle and build it more slowly than a 32-year-old man. The subject's 150–180 g/day of protein also protects lean mass better than the ~15%-protein diets in the Sardeli trials. So the *absolute* lean-mass loss that RT prevents is probably smaller for him. That RT beats walking for lean mass is still very likely true. [likely]
- **Post-meal walking works less on a low-carb diet.** The glucose benefit depends on how high glucose rises after a meal. Reynolds found the biggest effect after carb-heavy evening meals in T2D. At 30–60 g carbohydrate a day, the subject's glucose rises little after meals, so there is less for walking to lower. The case for walking here rests on total activity and insulin sensitivity, not on glucose after meals. [likely]
- **The RT–insulin-resistance meta-analysis may have a reporting error.** Its fasting-insulin result is given as "−1.03 (95% CI −1.03, −0.75)", with the point estimate sitting on one end of the interval. The HOMA-IR result looks internally consistent. I could not check its units or which studies it included. The subject's HOMA-IR of 3.58 is in the range where a −1 change would matter.
- **Weight-loss discrepancy.** A reported 9 kg in 5 weeks, compared with the ~6 kg implied by the BMI change, fits with early low-carb loss being partly glycogen and water. DXA counts water as lean mass, so early "lean loss" readings on this diet will look worse than the true loss of muscle.
- **Adherence studies are mostly not of entrepreneurs.** Project Active studied sedentary healthy adults. Lally studied volunteers, many of them students. Nothing I found studied people with 12-hour irregular days.

## Sources
### 1. Sardeli 2018: RT during caloric restriction
- **Citation:** Sardeli AV, Komatsu TR, Mori MA, Gáspari AF, Chacon-Mikahil MPT. 2018. ## Disagreements between sources
- **Home vs supervised RT.** The T2D meta-analysis found supervised gym RT did better than home RT. The subject's own history is that supervised programmes collapse. A dose done at home beats a better dose he doesn't do, but home RT may underdose load. Progression has to be deliberate.
- **Aerobic exercise and lean mass.** Villareal and Beavers found aerobic training *increased* lean loss compared with RT (Beavers: −1.6 vs −1.0 kg on diet alone). Donnelly frames aerobic activity as neutral. For lean mass, walking is not protective.
- **Weight loss.** Donnelly says RT does not boost weight loss. Beavers found WL+RT lost 3 kg more than WL alone over 18 months. The difference may be due to adherence or population.
- **Frequency.** Schoenfeld 2016 favoured 2×/week, but in studies that did not hold volume equal. Schoenfeld 2019 found no difference when volume was equal. Practically, 1×/week works only if that one session carries enough hard sets.
- **Energy compensation.** Pontzer predicts that part of walking's calorie burn is offset. Beavers still found extra fat loss with added exercise.

## Applying it to the subject
**Month-one priority: RT, done so that it builds a habit.** The case: (1) RT is the only proven lever for lean mass during a deficit (Sardeli, Villareal). (2) RT independently lowers HOMA-IR (−1.05 in obese adults without diabetes), and he is at 3.58. (3) On 30–60 g carb/day, the glucose benefit of post-meal walking is small. (4) Steps can be gathered without a scheduled block (Project Active), but RT cannot. So the one fixed slot should go to RT. The case against: walking is easier, and his record with structured sessions is poor (57% attendance, collapse by month 3). The way through: equal-volume frequency doesn't matter for hypertrophy (Schoenfeld 2019), and habits form faster with frequent, cued repetitions (Lally, Kaushal & Rhodes). So weekly RT volume can be split into short daily home "snacks", with no gym travel.

**Minimum viable prescription (weeks 1–12):**
1. **RT at home:** 4–5 days/week, 8–12 min each, tied to one fixed cue (for example, "after I get home, before I shower"). Rotate squat or split-squat, push-up (incline if needed), row (band or backpack), hip hinge, and plank. Do 2–3 hard sets, ending about 1–3 reps short of failure. Target ~6–10 hard sets per muscle group per week. **Confidence: medium.** Frequency and total volume are well supported. Home and bodyweight loading, and the snack format, are only pilot-tested for hypertrophy. The set target is extrapolated.
2. **Step target, not walk sessions:** measure 7 days of baseline, then aim for baseline + ~2,000/day, rising towards 7,000–9,000/day. Use phone or wearable tracking with a visible goal. **Confidence: medium-high** for adding steps (Bravata). Low for fat-loss effect beyond the diet (Donnelly, Pontzer).
3. **Post-meal walk of 10 min** after the largest meal when possible. **Confidence: low** for glucose benefit on his diet. Medium as an easy way to add steps.
4. **If-then plans** written for 2–3 predictable disruptions (for example, "If service runs past 22:00, I do 1 set of each on the kitchen floor"). **Confidence: medium** (d ≈ 0.24–0.31).
5. **Missed-day rule:** a missed day does not reset anything (Lally). Judge adherence over 4-week blocks, not daily streaks. **Confidence: medium.**

**Floor for busy weeks:** 2 RT snacks/week of about 10 min, with 2 hard sets each of squat, push-up and row. Keep daily steps at or above baseline (no drop below ~5,000). This keeps the habit cue alive and holds some stimulus. **Confidence: low-medium.** No trial tested this exact floor. It is extrapolated from minimal-dose reviews (Androulakis-Korakakis, Fyfe) and the finding that RT spares lean mass even in short trials.

**Worked estimate:** Suppose diet alone would cost ~1 kg of lean mass over 6 months (Beavers' diet-only arm lost −1.0 kg over 18 months, in older adults). RT at an adequate dose might spare most of that (Sardeli: ~0.8 kg, 93%). With 150–180 g of protein a day, his baseline loss is probably smaller, so the absolute benefit may be only ~0.3–0.8 kg. That gain is still real, and RT also brings insulin-sensitivity and strength benefits. [guessing]

Confidence: **medium**, because the direction (RT for lean mass, steps for sustainable activity) is consistent across RCTs and meta-analyses. However, the lean-mass trials are in adults ~35 years older on lower-protein, higher-carb diets, the minimal home-RT dose has not been tested for hypertrophy during a diet, and every number was checked only against indexed abstracts because full-text access was blocked.

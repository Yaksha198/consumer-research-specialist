## Plain-language answer
The coach's "~3 g water per g glycogen" is about right as a working number, and it is better described as **3 to 4 g water per g glycogen** [likely]. The human data behind it are thin, all come from lean and mostly trained men, and all are measured indirectly. The ratio is also not a fixed chemical constant. When extra water is available, far more than 3 g may be stored alongside each gram of glycogen, and most of the "glycogen water" is ordinary cell water that behaves osmotically like any other [likely]. Counting the glycogen itself, **each 100 g of glycogen gained or lost moves body weight by about 0.4–0.5 kg** [likely]. For this subject, who eats 30–60 g of carbohydrate a day, is insulin-resistant and does no training, one higher-carb day (150–300 g carbohydrate) probably stores about 100–250 g of glycogen. That is roughly **0.4–1.2 kg** of scale weight [guessing]. It can plausibly account for part of a 1–1.5 kg overnight jump, but usually not all of it. Gut contents (the weight of the food itself) and insulin-driven renal sodium/water retention are separate and additive contributors [likely].

## Insulin resistance / population caveat
- **Every ratio study used lean, healthy, mostly trained men**, and most started glycogen-depleted by exercise. None looked at obese or insulin-resistant people, or at anyone on a chronic 30–60 g/day intake. The ratio probably holds per gram because it reflects cell water and osmotic physics, but **how many grams get stored** will differ [likely].
- **Insulin resistance slows muscle glycogen storage.** Under matched hyperglycaemic-hyperinsulinaemic clamp conditions, people with type 2 diabetes synthesised muscle glycogen at 78±28 vs 183±39 µmol/kg muscle/min, about 43% of the normal rate (Shulman 1990). The subject is insulin-resistant, not diabetic, so his impairment is probably milder. Either way, a single refeed will likely store **less** glycogen than the lean-athlete studies suggest [likely].
- **No exercise means no exercise-driven boost to glycogen synthesis.** Every loading study used depletion exercise first. Sedentary refeeding is slower [likely].
- **Chronic low carb:** keto-adapted elite endurance athletes (Volek 2016) had resting muscle glycogen similar to high-carb athletes. This cannot be carried over to a sedentary, insulin-resistant man, whose muscle glycogen is not being drawn down by training and who has no training stimulus to rebuild it. For him the liver is the clearer effect: it drops to about 10–25% of normal within 24 h on a carb-poor diet and stays there (Nilsson & Hultman 1973) [likely].
- Liver and muscle mass are larger in obesity, so absolute stores may be larger than in lean people, not smaller [guessing].

## Sources
### 1. Olsson & Saltin 1970
- **Citation:** Olsson KE, Saltin B. 1970. Variation in total body water with muscle glycogen changes in man. *Acta Physiol Scand* 80(1):11–18. DOI 10.1111/j.1748-1716.1970.tb04764.x; PMID 5475323. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5475323/ (abstract only, via index)
- **Population:** 19 subjects (sex and body composition not stated in the abstract; the era suggests healthy young men), depleted by heavy arm and leg exercise and then 3 days of protein/fat diet.  **Mismatch vs subject:** lean, exercise-depleted, no insulin resistance.
- **Finding / number:** Carb loading for 4 days raised thigh glycogen from 4.5 to 19.9 g/kg and arm glycogen from 2.6 to 16.9 g/kg. Body weight rose 2.4 kg and total body water rose 2.2 L (tritium dilution), which the authors attributed to glycogen storage. Later reviews read this as 3–4 g water per g glycogen. **The frequently quoted "2.7 g" does not appear in the abstract.** Its origin is unclear and is UNVERIFIED as an Olsson & Saltin number.
- **Quality / limitations:** The ratio is indirect: whole-body water is divided by glycogen extrapolated from biopsies, and liver glycogen was not measured.

### 2. Fernández-Elías et al. 2015
- **Citation:** Fernández-Elías VE, Ortega JF, Nelson RK, Mora-Rodriguez R. 2015. Relationship between muscle water and glycogen recovery after prolonged exercise in the heat in humans. *Eur J Appl Physiol* 115:1919–1926. DOI 10.1007/s00421-015-3175-z; PMID 25911631. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25911631/ (abstract only, via index)
- **Population:** 9 aerobically trained subjects, dehydrated by 4.6% during 150 min of cycling in the heat.  **Mismatch vs subject:** trained, lean, dehydrated and depleted, acute setting.
- **Finding / number:** Recovery with 250 g carbohydrate in 400 mL water stored about 3 g water per g glycogen (1:3). The same carbohydrate plus about 3.17 L water gave about 1:17. The authors conclude "at least 3 g" per g glycogen, and attribute the extra water to storage not bound to glycogen.
- **Quality / limitations:** Small sample, and this is the only direct biopsy test of the ratio. It shows the ratio depends on how much water is taken in.

### 3. Kreitzman, Coxon & Szaz 1992
- **Citation:** Kreitzman SN, Coxon AY, Szaz KF. 1992. Glycogen storage: illusions of easy weight loss, excessive weight regain, and distortions in estimates of body composition. *Am J Clin Nutr* 56(1 Suppl):292S–293S. PMID 1615908. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1615908/ (abstract only, via index; DOI not seen)
- **Population:** 11 dieters on a VLCD for 4 days (presumably obese).  **Mismatch vs subject:** VLCD rather than low carb at about 2000 kcal. Closer in adiposity to the subject.
- **Finding / number:** Glycogen is stored in liver, muscle and fat cells with 3–4 parts water and 0.45 mmol K/g. Early changes in total body potassium mainly reflect glycogen. The paper warns about "exaggerated regain" when carbohydrate is loaded again. The abstract gives no numeric glycogen mass for the 11 subjects.
- **Quality / limitations:** A two-page conference supplement. The 3–4 figure is asserted rather than newly measured.

### 4. Nilsson & Hultman 1973 (liver)
- **Citation:** Nilsson LH, Hultman E. 1973. Liver glycogen in man — the effect of total starvation or a carbohydrate-poor diet followed by carbohydrate refeeding. *Scand J Clin Lab Invest* 32(4):325–330. DOI 10.3109/00365517309084355 (abstract only, via index; PMID not seen)
- **Population:** 19 human subjects, repeated percutaneous liver biopsies.  **Mismatch vs subject:** healthy, and adiposity is unknown.
- **Finding / number:** Liver glycogen was 232 mmol/kg after an overnight fast. It fell to 24–55 within 24 h of starvation or a carb-poor diet, stayed low for up to 9 more days, and rebounded to 424–624 mmol/kg on refeeding.
- **Quality / limitations:** Old, invasive and small, but still the standing human biopsy reference.

### Supporting / conflicting sources
- **Yang & Van Itallie 1976**, *J Clin Invest* 58(3):722–730, https://www.jci.org/articles/view/108519 (abstract only, via index; DOI/PMID not seen). 6 obese subjects in 10-day periods. The 800-kcal ketogenic diet lost 466.6 g/day, of which 61.2% was water. The isocaloric mixed diet lost 277.9 g/day, 37.1% water. **This is the closest population match.**
- **King, Jones & O'Hara 2018**, *Eur J Appl Physiol*, "The availability of water associated with glycogen during dehydration: a reservoir or raindrop?" DOI 10.1007/s00421-017-3768-9; PMID 29196846 (abstract only, via index; volume/pages UNVERIFIED). At about 2% glycogen concentration, glycogen-associated water is fully osmotically available. At 5–10%, only about 10–20% of it is "protected". It is not a separate reservoir. (This is probably the "King et al. 2018" the coach half-remembered. It is in EJAP, not J Appl Physiol.)
- **Sherman et al. 1982**, *Int J Sports Med* 3:22–24, DOI 10.1055/s-2008-1026056, PMID 7068293. **Conflicting.** In rats, muscle glycogen ranged from 10.0 to 30.2 µmol/g, and muscle water "showed no consistent relationship" to glycogen. This is rodent data.
- **Shiose et al. 2016**, *J Appl Physiol* 121:205–211, DOI 10.1152/japplphysiol.00126.2016. n=8, 12 g/kg/day carbohydrate for 72 h, 13C-MRS glycogen and deuterium total body water. Total body water rose, mainly as intracellular water. The introduction cites 2.7–4 g/g. Numeric results were not seen.
- **Bone et al. 2017**, *Med Sci Sports Exerc*, DOI 10.1249/MSS.0000000000001174, PMID 28410328. Glycogen loading in 18 trained cyclists raised total body water by 2.3% and DXA "lean mass" by 2–3%. Glycogen swings therefore distort body-composition scans.
- **Shulman et al. 1990**, *NEJM* 322:223–228, DOI 10.1056/NEJM199001253220403, PMID 2403659. Numbers are under the caveat above.
- **Acheson et al. 1988**, *Am J Clin Nutr*, PMID 3165600 (volume/pages UNVERIFIED). n=3 men. Glycogen capacity is about 15 g/kg body weight, and roughly 500 g can be gained before net lipogenesis begins.
- **Murray & Rosenbloom 2018**, *Nutr Rev* 76(4):243–259, DOI 10.1093/nutrit/nuy001. Whole-body glycogen is about 600 g (the figure was seen only in an index summary).
- **Volek et al. 2016**, *Metabolism*, PMID 26892521 (volume/pages UNVERIFIED). n=20 elite ultra-endurance athletes on about 20 months of low carb. Resting muscle glycogen was not different from high-carb athletes.

## Disagreements between sources
- **The ratio itself:** Olsson & Saltin and Kreitzman give 3–4. Fernández-Elías found "at least 3" and up to 17 with free water. Sherman found no consistent relationship in rats. King argues most of the water is not specifically "bound". **Resolution:** 3–4 g/g is a reasonable minimum for scale-weight estimates. The upper end is set by hydration and sodium, not by glycogen chemistry.
- **The "2.7 g" figure** is widely repeated. It does not appear in the Olsson & Saltin abstract and was seen only as part of a "2.7–4" range in Shiose 2016. Its original source is unconfirmed.
- **Low-carb baseline:** Volek (athletes) found normal muscle glycogen on keto. Nilsson & Hultman found the liver collapses on a carb-poor diet. These are compatible, because muscle and liver respond differently. For a sedentary person it is unknown whether muscle glycogen stays normal.

## Applying it to the subject
Assumptions: about 100–110 kg, skeletal muscle about 35 kg [guessing], liver about 1.8 kg [guessing].

1. **Mixed-diet stores:** muscle about 35 kg × 13–16 g/kg ≈ 450–560 g, plus liver about 1.8 kg × ~38 g/kg ≈ 70 g. Total ≈ **520–630 g** [likely].
2. **Chronic 30–60 g/day stores:** liver ≈ 7–16 g (4–9 g/kg). Muscle is uncertain: perhaps 250–450 g [guessing]. The total is about **260–470 g**, roughly 150–350 g below mixed-diet levels.
3. **Weight effect of the initial switch to low carb:** 150–350 g glycogen × 4–5 (1 g glycogen plus 3–4 g water) ≈ **0.6–1.75 kg**. Add the natriuresis of carb restriction (see sodium topic). Yang & Van Itallie's 61% water share is consistent with this. Of a reported 9 kg loss in 5 weeks, perhaps 1–2.5 kg is glycogen-linked water plus sodium-linked water [likely]. (The BMI change 35 to 33 implies only about 6 kg at typical height, so this discrepancy should be checked. If the true loss is about 6 kg, the water share is proportionally larger.)
4. **One higher-carb day, worked estimate** (ratio 3–4 g/g, glycogen mass included):
   - +150 g glycogen: 150 × 4 to 150 × 5 = **0.60–0.75 kg**
   - +300 g glycogen: 300 × 4 to 300 × 5 = **1.2–1.5 kg**
   - Storing 300 g of glycogen in one day, with insulin resistance and no exercise, probably needs well over 400 g of carbohydrate. A 150–300 g carbohydrate day more plausibly stores about 100–200 g, which is **0.4–1.0 kg** [guessing].
5. **Maximum plausible swing** from low-carb baseline to fully loaded (about 800–1,000 g), counting only glycogen and its water: ≈ (400–700 g) × 4–5 ≈ **1.6–3.5 kg**. Olsson & Saltin measured 2.4 kg empirically [likely].
6. **Confound:** refeeding raises insulin, which increases renal sodium reabsorption and so retains extracellular water. This is a separate mechanism from glycogen-bound (intracellular) water, and it is *not* included in the 3–4 g/g ratio. Glycogen storage also takes up potassium (0.45 mmol/g). Undigested food and fibre in the gut add their own mass. A 1–1.5 kg overnight gain after a refeed is therefore likely a mix of about 0.4–1.0 kg glycogen water, plus sodium-retained water, plus gut contents. It should not be read as fat gain [likely].

Confidence: medium. The 3–4 g/g ratio agrees across sources and matches the whole-body data, but every ratio study is in lean, trained, exercise-depleted men. The subject's actual stores and his storage rate under insulin resistance are estimated, not measured. And no source could be fetched in full for this write-up.

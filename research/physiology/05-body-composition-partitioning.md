## Plain-language answer
When overweight adults diet without resistance training, the lean ("fat-free") share of the weight they lose is not a fixed 25%. In the first weeks it is often about 50–60%, falling to about 25–35% by 6 months. What drives it: higher starting body fat lowers the lean share; men show a higher lean share than women; faster or more severe restriction raises it; a low-carb start raises it, mostly through glycogen-bound water. Higher protein (roughly >1.05–1.6 g/kg) reliably reduces lean loss by only about 0.4–0.6 kg compared with standard protein. That makes it *lean-sparing*, not *lean-building*. The well-known "lean gain in a deficit" result (Longland 2016) needed six days a week of resistance plus interval training. I found no evidence that high protein alone produces measurable lean gain during a large deficit, even in people starting above 30% body fat. A bicep circumference rise over one week with no training is most plausibly measurement error or fluid, not new muscle.

## Insulin resistance / population caveat
- **Low-carb confound:** Krieger's meta-regression and Hall's ketogenic metabolic-ward study both found *more* measured FFM loss on low-carb or ketogenic diets. Glycogen is stored with 3–4 parts water (Kreitzman 1992), and DXA and BIA count that water as "lean". So on this subject's diet (~30–60 g carb/day), early apparent lean loss is inflated by water, not muscle protein [likely]. The opposite also holds: any carb refeed will show as a "lean gain".
- **Insulin resistance itself:** I found no fetched source on whether insulin resistance changes the lean fraction during a deficit. The idea of "anabolic resistance" in obesity is plausible but **UNVERIFIED** here.
- **Protein studies are mostly short, supervised trials** in young, often normal-weight or active adults (Pasiakos, Longland) or adults over 50 (Kim). None matches a 32-year-old Class II obese, insulin-resistant man on a self-directed plan.
- **Sex:** Heymsfield's data give men a *higher* early lean fraction than women, which is unfavourable for this male subject.

## Sources
### 1. Heymsfield et al. 2014 – "quarter FFM rule" critique
- **Citation:** Heymsfield SB, Gonzalez MC, Shen W, Redman L, Thomas D. 2014. Weight loss composition is one-fourth fat-free mass: a critical review and critique of this widely cited rule. *Obes Rev* 15(4):310–321. DOI 10.1111/obr.12143; PMID 24447775. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24447775/ (search-index abstract; page not fetched)
- **Population:** Review of hypocaloric diet and exercise studies, including CALERIE and VLCD trials; mixed sexes and adiposity. **Mismatch vs subject:** Mostly supervised trials, not low-carb; applies directly to diet-only loss.
- **Finding / number:** The FFM share is time-dependent: 0.60 (men) and 0.50 (women) at week 4, falling to about 0.35 and 0.24 at week 24. The 0.25 rule describes longer-term loss, not the early weeks.
- **Quality / limitations:** Authoritative review. The week-by-week numbers come from index text of the full article, not a fetched page.

### 2. Forbes 2000 / Hall 2007 – starting fat sets the lean share
- **Citation:** Forbes GB. 2000. Body fat content influences the body composition response to nutrition and exercise. *Ann N Y Acad Sci* 904:359–365. DOI 10.1111/j.1749-6632.2000.tb06482.x; PMID 10865771. Hall KD. 2007. Body fat and fat-free mass inter-relationships: Forbes's theory revisited. *Br J Nutr* 97(6):1059–1063. PMID 17367567. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17367567/ (both search-index abstract; pages not fetched)
- **Population:** Pooled human data plus a mathematical model. **Mismatch vs subject:** Forbes's curve was built largely from women's data; it is a population model, not an individual prediction.
- **Finding / number:** Forbes found an inverse, curvilinear relationship between starting body fat and the lean share of weight lost. In overfeeding, weight gained is 60–70% lean in thin people but 30–40% in obese people. Hall extended the equation to large weight changes: the lean share depends on starting composition *and* on the size and direction of the change. The commonly quoted form, ΔFFM/ΔW ≈ 10.4/(10.4 + fat mass in kg), is from memory and **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Quality / limitations:** Standing theoretical reference. It says nothing about protein, rate of loss or diet type.

### 3. Wycherley et al. 2012 and Krieger et al. 2006 – what protein buys
- **Citation:** Wycherley TP, Moran LJ, Clifton PM, Noakes M, Brinkworth GD. 2012. Effects of energy-restricted high-protein, low-fat compared with standard-protein, low-fat diets: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. *Am J Clin Nutr* 96(6):1281–1298. PMID 23097268. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23097268/ — Krieger JW, Sitren HS, Daniels MJ, Langkamp-Henken B. 2006. Effects of variation in protein and carbohydrate intake on body mass and composition during energy restriction: a meta-regression. *Am J Clin Nutr* 83(2):260–274. PMID 16469983. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16469983/ (both search-index abstract; pages not fetched)
- **Population:** Wycherley: 24 RCTs, n=1,063 overweight or obese adults, 12.1 ± 9.3 wk. Krieger: 87 studies, 165 groups, adults ≥19 y, intake ≥1,000 kcal/day, ≥4 wk. **Mismatch vs subject:** Wycherley covers low-fat diets, not low-carb, and its "high" protein arm (1.07–1.60 g/kg) sits at or below the subject's intake. Deficits were mostly moderate.
- **Finding / number:** Wycherley: high protein kept 0.43 kg more FFM and lost 0.87 kg more fat. Krieger: protein >1.05 g/kg kept 0.60 kg more FFM; low-carb diets showed 0.69 kg *more* FFM loss. Krieger's resistance-training effect size was not confirmed and is omitted.
- **Quality / limitations:** Meta-analyses of heterogeneous, short trials. The effect is sub-kilogram and still means net lean *loss*, just less of it. The 95% CIs were not retrieved.

### 4. Longland et al. 2016 – the "lean gain in a deficit" study (training included)
- **Citation:** Longland TM, Oikawa SY, Mitchell CJ, Devries MC, Phillips SM. 2016. Higher compared with lower dietary protein during an energy deficit combined with intense exercise promotes greater lean mass gain and fat mass loss: a randomized trial. *Am J Clin Nutr* 103(3):738–746. DOI 10.3945/ajcn.115.119339; PMID 26817506. (search-index abstract; page not fetched; volume, pages and author list beyond Longland/Phillips are **UNVERIFIED**)
- **Population:** 40 young men, overweight/recreationally active. **Mismatch vs subject:** Large. They trained 6 d/wk (full-body RT plus HIIT) under supervision, with food provided.
- **Finding / number:** On a 40% deficit for 4 wk, the 2.4 g/kg group gained +1.2 kg LBM and lost −4.8 kg fat; the 1.2 g/kg group gained +0.1 kg LBM and lost −3.5 kg fat.
- **Quality / limitations:** Well controlled but short. It is **not** evidence for protein alone.

**Supporting / conflicting sources (all search-index; pages not fetched):**
- *Vink et al. 2016*, *Obesity*, DOI 10.1002/oby.21346, PMID 26813524. n=57, BMI 28–35. A 500 kcal VLCD for 5 wk (−9.0 kg) caused much more FFM loss than a 1,250 kcal diet for 12 wk (−8.2 kg): 8.8% vs 1.3%. It is unclear whether the denominator is baseline FFM or weight lost.
- *Ashtary-Larky et al. 2020*, *Br J Nutr* 124(11). Meta-analysis of 7 studies: FFM did not differ between rapid and gradual loss; gradual loss removed 1 kg more fat and better preserved RMR.
- *Pasiakos et al. 2013*, *FASEB J* 27:3837–3847, DOI 10.1096/fj.13-230227, PMID 23739654. n=39, 21 d at a 40% deficit. Protein at 1.6 or 2.4 g/kg (vs 0.8) gave a lower FFM share of weight lost; 2.4 added no benefit over 1.6. Population details and the exercise component are **UNVERIFIED**; exact percentages were not retrieved.
- *Kim et al. 2016*, *Nutr Rev* 74(3):210–224, PMID 26883880. 20 RCTs, adults >50 y: higher protein kept more lean mass and lost more fat. The commonly quoted "+0.60 kg lean" figure is **UNVERIFIED**.
- *Hall et al. 2016*, *Am J Clin Nutr* 104(2):324–333, DOI 10.3945/ajcn.116.133561, PMID 27385608. 17 overweight/obese men on a metabolic ward: switching to a ketogenic diet slowed fat loss and coincided with FFM loss on DXA.
- *Garthe et al. 2011*, *IJSNEM* 21(2):97–104, PMID 21558571. Elite athletes doing 4 RT sessions/wk: losing 0.7% BW/wk gave +2.1% LBM and −31% fat; losing 1.4%/wk gave −21% fat.
- *Kreitzman et al. 1992*, *Am J Clin Nutr* 56(1 Suppl):292S, PMID 1615908. Glycogen is stored with 3–4 parts water; glycogen shifts distort body-composition estimates.
- *Tinsley & Willoughby 2016*, *IJSNEM* 26(1):78–92. Early ketogenic-diet weight loss is glycogen and water.
- *Hector & Phillips 2018*, *IJSNEM* 28(2):170–177, DOI 10.1123/ijsnem.2017-0273, PMID 29182451. Recommends 1.6–2.4 g/kg for athletes in a deficit, with training.
- *Ulijaszek & Kerr 1999*, *Br J Nutr* 82(3):165–177, PMID 10655963. Circumferences are less precise than weight or height, and one observer should take them. The paper's arm-specific TEM was not retrieved.

## Disagreements between sources
- **Rate of loss:** Vink 2016 found much higher FFM loss with rapid loss; the Ashtary-Larky 2020 meta-analysis found no significant FFM difference, only less fat lost. Garthe found the rate mattered even with training. Likely explanations: method (water-sensitive measures), whether a weight-stable period came before measurement, and VLCD severity.
- **"25%" vs. "50–60%":** These are not in conflict. They describe different time points (Heymsfield 2014). People who quote 25% for a 5-week result are applying the wrong time scale.
- **Low-carb and FFM:** Krieger and Hall show more *measured* FFM loss on low-carb or ketogenic diets. Tinsley and Kreitzman argue much of it is water. Neither side cleanly separates water from muscle protein.

## Applying it to the subject
Assume height about 1.78 m [guessing]. BMI 35→33 then corresponds to about 6.3 kg, not 9 kg. Energy balance favours the smaller figure: intake ~2,050 kcal against a TDEE of roughly 3,000–3,300 kcal [guessing] is a deficit of about 1,000–1,200 kcal/day, or 35,000–42,000 kcal over 5 weeks. That is about 4.5–5.5 kg of fat tissue at most [likely].

Worked split of the claimed 9 kg:
- Glycogen plus bound water from going low-carb: ~1.5–2.5 kg (glycogen stores ~400–500 g, **UNVERIFIED**, × 3–4 water).
- Fat: ~4.5–5.5 kg.
- Remaining ~1–3 kg: other water or true FFM.

On DXA or BIA the "lean" share of the loss could show as 35–55%, matching Heymsfield's week-4 values. The true muscle-protein share is probably much smaller [guessing].

High protein here (~1.4–1.7 g/kg of actual body weight) probably saved a few hundred grams of lean mass compared with standard protein [likely]. It did **not** build muscle [likely].

**Bicep:** Losing fat should *shrink* arm girth. A one-week increase is best explained by:
- tape error, which is about ±0.5–1 cm even for trained measurers [likely];
- differences in tape position or tension, flexed vs relaxed arm, or time of day;
- fluid shifts, including carb or salt intake.

It is not evidence of recomposition [likely]. To test it: take three measurements, relaxed arm, at a marked mid-point, same time of day, and track the 2–4-week trend.

Confidence: **medium**, because the direction (protein spares lean mass but does not build it; the lean share is high early and on low-carb) is consistent across sources. But every citation was checked only through search-index abstracts, several specific numbers are **UNVERIFIED**, and no study matches an untrained, insulin-resistant, low-carb Class II obese man.

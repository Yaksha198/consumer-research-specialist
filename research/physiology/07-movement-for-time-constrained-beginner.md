# Topic 7: Best movement combination for a time-constrained beginner

## Plain-language answer
During a diet, resistance training (RT) is the only exercise shown to reliably protect lean mass. In dieting obese older adults, RT prevented about 0.8 kg (≈93%) of the lean mass that diet alone lost. Walking and aerobic work do not do this, and in one trial they made lean-mass loss worse. The RT dose that works is small. When weekly volume is held equal, training a muscle once, twice or three times a week gives similar growth, so a short session at home counts. Walking is the easier habit to keep. More steps are linked to lower mortality, with benefit levelling off at about 8,000–10,000 steps/day under age 60. Short walks after meals lower glucose after eating, but mostly after carb-heavy meals, and this subject eats very little carbohydrate. The adherence evidence decides the plan. About half of people drop out of structured programmes within 6 months. Lifestyle activity worked as well as a gym prescription over 24 months. Step tracking with a goal adds about 2,500 steps/day. Habits form slowly (median 66 days) and survive a missed day. **Recommendation:** in month one, make home RT the one fixed commitment, done as short daily "snacks" or 2 × ~20-min sessions. Treat walking as a step target filled in around the day, not as a scheduled session. [likely]

## Insulin resistance / population caveat
- **Lean-mass trials are in older adults.** Sardeli (six RCTs), Villareal and Beavers (mean age ~67) all studied people aged about 65–70. Those people start with less muscle and build it more slowly than a 32-year-old man. The subject's 150–180 g/day of protein also protects lean mass better than the ~15%-protein diets in the Sardeli trials. So the *absolute* lean-mass loss that RT prevents is probably smaller for him. That RT beats walking for lean mass is still very likely true. [likely]
- **Post-meal walking works less on a low-carb diet.** The glucose benefit depends on how high glucose rises after a meal. Reynolds found the biggest effect after carb-heavy evening meals in T2D. At 30–60 g carbohydrate a day, the subject's glucose rises little after meals, so there is less for walking to lower. The case for walking here rests on total activity and insulin sensitivity, not on glucose after meals. [likely]
- **The RT–insulin-resistance meta-analysis may have a reporting error.** Its fasting-insulin result is given as "−1.03 (95% CI −1.03, −0.75)", with the point estimate sitting on one end of the interval. The HOMA-IR result looks internally consistent. I could not check its units or which studies it included. The subject's HOMA-IR of 3.58 is in the range where a −1 change would matter.
- **Weight-loss discrepancy.** A reported 9 kg in 5 weeks, compared with the ~6 kg implied by the BMI change, fits with early low-carb loss being partly glycogen and water. DXA counts water as lean mass, so early "lean loss" readings on this diet will look worse than the true loss of muscle.
- **Adherence studies are mostly not of entrepreneurs.** Project Active studied sedentary healthy adults. Lally studied volunteers, many of them students. Nothing I found studied people with 12-hour irregular days.

## Sources
### 1. Sardeli 2018: RT during caloric restriction
- **Citation:** Sardeli AV, Komatsu TR, Mori MA, Gáspari AF, Chacon-Mikahil MPT. 2018. Resistance Training Prevents Muscle Loss Induced by Caloric Restriction in Obese Elderly Individuals: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis. *Nutrients* 10(4):423. DOI 10.3390/nu10040423; PMID 29596307. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29596307/ (abstract only)
- **Population:** 6 RCTs, obese elderly, DXA; RT 3×/week for 12–24 weeks; diets mostly 55% carbohydrate / 15% protein / 30% fat. **Mismatch vs subject:** older; lower protein; supervised 3×/week RT; high-carb diets.
- **Finding / number:** RT prevented 93.5% of the lean mass lost to caloric restriction, a difference of 0.819 kg (0.364–1.273). Fat and total mass losses were similar with or without RT.
- **Quality / limitations:** Only 6 small trials, all in the elderly and all at 3×/week. Says nothing about lower doses.

### 2. Villareal 2017: aerobic vs RT vs combined while dieting
- **Citation:** Villareal DT, et al. 2017. Aerobic or Resistance Exercise, or Both, in Dieting Obese Older Adults. *N Engl J Med* 376(20):1943–1955. DOI 10.1056/NEJMoa1616338; PMID 28514618. (abstract only)
- **Population:** 160 obese adults aged ≥65, frail, supervised, 26 weeks. **Mismatch vs subject:** age, frailty, supervised gym setting.
- **Finding / number:** Lean mass fell 5% with aerobic exercise (55.0→52.3 kg), 2% with RT (58.1→57.1 kg) and 3% with combined training (56.5→54.8 kg). The Physical Performance Test score rose 21% with combined training vs 14% with either alone.
- **Quality / limitations:** Strong RCT, but high-dose supervised training (~3×/week) that the subject has already failed to keep up.

### 3. Schoenfeld, Grgic, Krieger 2019: training frequency and hypertrophy
- **Citation:** Schoenfeld BJ, Grgic J, Krieger J. 2019. How many times per week should a muscle be trained to maximize muscle hypertrophy? A systematic review and meta-analysis. *J Sports Sci* 37(11). DOI 10.1080/02640414.2018.1555906; PMID 30558493. Page numbers UNVERIFIED. (abstract only)
- **Population:** 25 studies, mostly young healthy adults, trained and untrained. **Mismatch vs subject:** not dieting, not obese or insulin-resistant.
- **Finding / number:** With weekly volume held equal, higher and lower frequencies did not differ significantly. The authors say people can choose frequency based on preference. The 2016 version (*Sports Med* 46(11):1689–1697, DOI 10.1007/s40279-016-0543-8) found 2×/week beat 1×/week, but most of those studies did not hold volume equal.
- **Quality / limitations:** Hypertrophy measures, short trials, calorie surplus or maintenance.

### 4. Paluch 2022: daily steps and mortality
- **Citation:** Paluch AE, et al. 2022. Daily steps and all-cause mortality: a meta-analysis of 15 international cohorts. *Lancet Public Health* 7(3):e219–e228. DOI 10.1016/S2468-2667(21)00302-9; PMC9289978. (abstract only; PMID not confirmed)
- **Population:** 47,471 adults, 3,013 deaths, median follow-up 7.1 years. **Mismatch vs subject:** general population, observational, outcome is mortality rather than body composition.
- **Finding / number:** Mortality benefit levels off at about 8,000–10,000 steps/day under 60 and 6,000–8,000 at 60+. The top quartiles had 40–53% lower risk. Stepping speed added little once total steps were accounted for.
- **Quality / limitations:** Observational, so reverse causation is possible (sick people walk less).

### Supporting / conflicting sources (abstract only unless noted)
- **Stens 2023**, *JACC*, DOI 10.1016/j.jacc.2023.07.029. 12 studies, n=111,309. Risk falls significantly from 2,517 steps/day. Peak benefit at 8,763 steps (all-cause mortality) and 7,126 (CVD).
- **Saint-Maurice 2020**, *JAMA* (volume/pages UNVERIFIED). n=4,840, US adults aged ≥40. HR 0.49 for 8,000 vs 4,000 steps/day. Step intensity not significant once total steps were adjusted for.
- **Beavers 2017**, *Obesity*, DOI 10.1002/oby.21977, PMID 29086504. n=249, mean age 66.9, BMI 34.4, 18 months. Weight loss: WL −5.7 kg, WL+aerobic −8.5 kg, WL+RT −8.7 kg. Fat loss: −4.8, −6.8 and −7.8 kg. Lean loss: −1.0, −1.6 and −0.8 kg.
- **RT and insulin resistance meta-analysis, 2023**, PMID 37331899 (authors and journal UNVERIFIED). 27 studies of overweight/obese adults without diabetes. HOMA-IR −1.05 (−1.33 to −0.76).
- **Home vs gym RT in T2D, 2025**, *Diabetol Metab Syndr*, DOI 10.1186/s13098-025-01793-7. 20 trials, n=1,397. Supervised gym RT lowered HbA1c more than home RT. The authors suggest this may be due to adherence, equipment and load-dosing differences. **This conflicts with the "home is enough" argument.**
- **Buffey 2022**, *Sports Med* 52(8):1765–1787, DOI 10.1007/s40279-022-01649-4, PMID 35147898. 7 acute crossover trials. Standing and light-walking breaks lowered postprandial glucose, with walking the better break. Mean 6-h glucose was 5–12% lower than sitting, and glucose area under the curve was 24% lower with walking. Insulin: no significant effect from standing.
- **Reynolds 2016**, *Diabetologia*, PMID 27747394 (volume/pages UNVERIFIED). n=41 with T2D, mean age 60. Ten minutes of walking after each meal beat 30 min at any time by 12%, and by 22% after the evening meal.
- **Donnelly 2009, ACSM position stand**, *MSSE*, DOI 10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181949333. Recommends 150–250 min/week for modest weight loss and >250 min/week to maintain losses. RT "does not enhance weight loss" but may increase fat-free mass.
- **Pontzer 2016**, *Curr Biol*, PMID 26832439. n=332, aged 25–45. Total energy expenditure rises with activity at low levels and then plateaus, so the calories burned by walking are partly compensated for.
- **Dunn 1999 (Project Active)**, *JAMA*, PMID 9929085 (volume/pages and effect sizes UNVERIFIED). n=235 sedentary healthy adults. Over 24 months, lifestyle activity was as effective as structured exercise for activity, VO2peak and blood pressure, and was more cost-effective.
- **Bravata 2007**, *JAMA*, PMID 18029834. Having a step goal was a key predictor of success.
- **Lally 2010**, *Eur J Soc Psychol* 40(6):998–1009, DOI 10.1002/ejsp.674. n=96, 82 analysed. The "exercise takes ~1.5× longer" figure comes from a BPS Research Digest summary.
- **Kaushal & Rhodes 2015**, *J Behav Med*, DOI 10.1007/s10865-015-9640-7, PMID 25851609. n=111 new gym members.
- **Bélanger-Gravel 2013**, *Health Psychol Rev* 7:23–54, DOI 10.1080/17437199.2011.560095. 26 studies.
- **Dishman ~50% dropout:** quoted second-hand in PMC5289308. Original not retrieved.
- **Islam, Gibala, Little 2022**, *Exerc Sport Sci Rev* 50(1):31–37. Defines "exercise snacks" as vigorous bouts of ≤1 min. Twenty-second stair climbs, 3 days/week for 6 weeks, improved fitness.
- **Home resistance "exercise snacking" pilot**, *BMC Geriatr* 2022, PMID 35751032. n=38, mean age 69.8. Nine-minute sessions 1–3×/day for 4 weeks were feasible. Hypertrophy outcomes not established.
- **Minimal-dose RT reviews:** Iversen 2021 *Sports Med* (PMID 34125411), Fyfe 2022 *Sports Med* 52(3):463–479 (PMID 34822137) and Androulakis-Korakakis 2020 *Sports Med* (PMID 31797219). The last found that a single set of 6–12 reps at ~70–85% 1RM, 2–3×/week, gives "suboptimal, yet significant" strength gains in trained men. Iversen's specific set and time prescriptions are UNVERIFIED.
- **Not retrieved (UNVERIFIED, not relied on):** Weiss 2017, Miller 2013, Banach 2023, Grgic 2018 effect sizes (citation only: *Sports Med*, DOI 10.1007/s40279-018-0872-x), Jenkins exercise-snack trials.

## Disagreements between sources
- **Home vs supervised RT.** The T2D meta-analysis found supervised gym RT did better than home RT. The subject's own history is that supervised programmes collapse. A dose done at home beats a better dose he doesn't do, but home RT may underdose load. Progression has to be deliberate.
- **Aerobic exercise and lean mass.** Villareal and Beavers found aerobic training *increased* lean loss compared with RT (Beavers: −1.6 vs −1.0 kg on diet alone). Donnelly frames aerobic activity as neutral. For lean mass, walking is not protective.
- **Weight loss.** Donnelly says RT does not boost weight loss. Beavers found WL+RT lost 3 kg more than WL alone over 18 months. The difference may be due to adherence or population.
- **Frequency.** Schoenfeld 2016 favoured 2×/week, but in studies that did not hold volume equal. Schoenfeld 2019 found no difference when volume was equal. Practically, 1×/week works only if that one session carries enough hard sets.
- **Energy compensation.** Pontzer predicts that part of walking's calorie burn is offset. Beavers still found extra fat loss with added exercise.

## Applying it to the subject
**Month-one priority: RT, done so that it builds a habit.** The case: (1) RT is the only proven lever for lean mass during a deficit (Sardeli, Villareal). (2) RT independently lowers HOMA-IR (−1.05 in obese adults without diabetes), and he is at 3.58. (3) On 30–60 g carb/day, the glucose benefit of post-meal walking is small. (4) Steps can be gathered without a scheduled block (Project Active), but RT cannot. So the one fixed slot should go to RT. The case against: walking is easier, and his record with structured sessions is poor (57% attendance, collapse by month 3). The way through: equal-volume frequency doesn't matter for hypertrophy (Schoenfeld 2019), and habits form faster with frequent, cued repetitions (Lally, Kaushal & Rhodes). So weekly RT volume can be split into short daily home "snacks", with no gym travel.

**Minimum viable prescription (weeks 1–12):**
1. **RT at home:** 4–5 days/week, 8–12 min each, tied to one fixed cue (for example, "after I get home, before I shower"). Rotate squat or split-squat, push-up (incline if needed), row (band or backpack), hip hinge, and plank. Do 2–3 hard sets, ending about 1–3 reps short of failure. Target ~6–10 hard sets per muscle group per week. **Confidence: medium.** Frequency and total volume are well supported. Home and bodyweight loading, and the snack format, are only pilot-tested for hypertrophy. The set target is extrapolated.
2. **Step target, not walk sessions:** measure 7 days of baseline, then aim for baseline + ~2,000/day, rising towards 7,000–9,000/day. Use phone or wearable tracking with a visible goal. **Confidence: medium-high** for adding steps (Bravata). Low for fat-loss effect beyond the diet (Donnelly, Pontzer).
3. **Post-meal walk of 10 min** after the largest meal when possible. **Confidence: low** for glucose benefit on his diet. Medium as an easy way to add steps.
4. **If-then plans** written for 2–3 predictable disruptions (for example, "If service runs past 22:00, I do 1 set of each on the kitchen floor"). **Confidence: medium** (d ≈ 0.24–0.31).
5. **Missed-day rule:** a missed day does not reset anything (Lally). Judge adherence over 4-week blocks, not daily streaks. **Confidence: medium.**

**Floor for busy weeks:** 2 RT snacks/week of about 10 min, with 2 hard sets each of squat, push-up and row. Keep daily steps at or above baseline (no drop below ~5,000). This keeps the habit cue alive and holds some stimulus. **Confidence: low-medium.** No trial tested this exact floor. It is extrapolated from minimal-dose reviews (Androulakis-Korakakis, Fyfe) and the finding that RT spares lean mass even in short trials.

**Worked estimate:** Suppose diet alone would cost ~1 kg of lean mass over 6 months (Beavers' diet-only arm lost −1.0 kg over 18 months, in older adults). RT at an adequate dose might spare most of that (Sardeli: ~0.8 kg, 93%). With 150–180 g of protein a day, his baseline loss is probably smaller, so the absolute benefit may be only ~0.3–0.8 kg. That gain is still real, and RT also brings insulin-sensitivity and strength benefits. [guessing]

Confidence: **medium**, because the direction (RT for lean mass, steps for sustainable activity) is consistent across RCTs and meta-analyses. However, the lean-mass trials are in adults ~35 years older on lower-protein, higher-carb diets, the minimal home-RT dose has not been tested for hypertrophy during a diet, and every number was checked only against indexed abstracts because full-text access was blocked.

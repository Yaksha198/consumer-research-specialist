## Plain-language answer
Three separate things are being run together here. (1) **Acute protein satiety** is real and well replicated. At ~25-30% of energy, protein reduces ad libitum intake on the same day and over weeks, and Weigle 2005 showed this with carbohydrate held at 50%, so no ketosis was involved [certain]. (2) **The protein leverage hypothesis (PLH)** says something else. Humans regulate absolute protein intake more tightly than energy, so a *low protein proportion* makes people overeat total energy. Its best-supported arm is the low-protein side [likely]. Whether intake falls *below* energy needs once the protein target is exceeded is contested: Gosby 2011 found no drop going from 15% to 25% protein, while Martens 2013/2014 found drops at 30% [likely contested]. (3) **A lagged, discrete "low day" after 2-3 high-protein days** is not a named or documented phenomenon. I found no study showing multi-day carryover of this kind. The main candidate mechanism, FGF21, is a signal of protein *deficit*, not protein excess [likely]. The subject also eats low-carb (~30-60 g/day), so mild ketosis is a live confound. Normal day-to-day intake varies with a CV of ~23-25%, and busy 12-hour workdays and missed meals add to that. So the coach's pattern is most likely **overfitting** [likely]. The protein is probably lowering his average intake, but it probably does not explain the particular low days.

## Insulin resistance / population caveat
- **Almost every PLH trial used lean or normal-weight people.** Gosby 2011 (n=22) was lean. Martens 2013 had a mean BMI of 23.7, Martens 2014 24.4, and Weigle 2005 used healthy non-obese adults. The only obese-male trial here, Johnstone 2008, is a ketogenic comparison. None studied insulin-resistant men at a BMI of ~33-35.
- **Most "high protein" arms kept carbohydrate moderate.** The subject's diet combines high protein with low carbohydrate, and it is closest to Johnstone 2008's ketogenic arm. There, cutting carbohydrate added a further ~0.7 MJ/d (~170 kcal) reduction beyond protein alone. On ~30-60 g carbohydrate/day, mild ketosis is plausible, particularly on low-intake days. **Ketosis therefore cannot be excluded as a confound.** Gibson 2015 found ketogenic low-carb diets modestly reduce hunger and desire to eat.
- **The subject's per-kg intake is below the question's threshold.** Take ~107 kg (BMI 35 at an assumed 1.75 m). Then 150-180 g/day is **~1.4-1.7 g/kg of actual weight**, not >2 g/kg. It would exceed 2 g/kg only per kg of lean or ideal weight. As a share of energy it is high: ~600-720 kcal of ~2000-2100, or **~29-35% of energy**, which is the range where Martens saw reduced intake.
- The FGF21 human data (Nicolaisen 2025) come from **lean** men on protein *restriction*. They say nothing direct about high intake in obesity.

## Sources
### 1. Weigle 2005 — acute and sustained protein satiety without carbohydrate restriction
- **Citation:** Weigle DS et al., 2005, "A high-protein diet induces sustained reductions in appetite, ad libitum caloric intake, and body weight despite compensatory changes in diurnal plasma leptin and ghrelin concentrations," *Am J Clin Nutr* 82(1):41-48. https://ajcn.nutrition.org/article/S0002-9165(23)29507-6/fulltext (PMID not confirmed)
- **Population:** n=19 healthy non-obese adults. Phases: 2 wk at 15% protein, 2 wk isocaloric at 30% protein, then 12 wk ad lib at 30%. Carbohydrate held at 50%.  **Mismatch vs subject:** not obese, not insulin-resistant, not low-carb.
- **Finding / number:** spontaneous intake fell 441 ± 63 kcal/d; weight −4.9 kg, fat −3.7 kg. This isolates protein from carbohydrate restriction.
- **Quality / limitations:** sequential rather than randomized design; small n; reports intake averages, not day-by-day lag.

### 2. Gosby 2011 and 2014 — the PLH tests (proportion, and the asymmetry question)
- **Citation:** Gosby AK et al., 2011, "Testing protein leverage in lean humans: a randomised controlled experimental study," *PLoS One* 6(10):e25929. DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0025929, PMID 22022472. Gosby AK, Conigrave AD, Raubenheimer D, Simpson SJ, 2014, "Protein leverage and energy intake," *Obes Rev* 15:183-191. DOI 10.1111/obr.12131. Theory: Simpson & Raubenheimer 2005, *Obes Rev* 6:133-142, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-789X.2005.00178.x. Raubenheimer & Simpson 2019, "Protein leverage: theoretical foundations and ten points of clarification," *Obesity*, DOI 10.1002/oby.22531, PMID 31339001.
- **Population:** 2011: n=22 lean adults, three 4-day in-house periods at 10/15/25% protein. 2014: 38 ad lib trials spanning 8-54% protein.  **Mismatch vs subject:** lean; short exposures; mostly not low-carb.
- **Finding / number:** 10% protein gave +12% energy over 4 days, 70% of it from snacking. **Going from 15% to 25% gave no change.** The 2014 meta-analysis found an inverse relation between % protein and energy intake. The 2019 theory paper says strong protein regulation causes overconsumption on low-protein diets and *underconsumption* on high-protein diets, with protein prioritised over non-protein energy. The effect is framed as a within-diet, proportion-driven steady state, **not a lagged next-day response**. The geometric-framework term "rule of compromise" for intake beyond the target is **UNVERIFIED** here, because no page defining it could be fetched.
- **Quality / limitations:** tightly controlled menus; short periods; the high-protein arm is where the evidence is weakest.

### 3. Martens 2013/2014 — evidence for the high-protein arm
- **Citation:** Martens EA, Lemmens SG, Westerterp-Plantenga MS, 2013, "Protein leverage affects energy intake of high-protein diets in humans," *Am J Clin Nutr* 97(1):86-93, PMID 23221572. Martens EA et al., 2014, "Protein leverage effects of beef protein on energy intake in humans," *Am J Clin Nutr* 99(6):1397-1406, PMID 24760974.
- **Population:** 2013: n=79 (40 M/39 F), age 34 ± 18, BMI 23.7 ± 3.4. 2014: n=58, age 33 ± 16, BMI 24.4 ± 4.0. Both were 12-day randomized crossovers at 5/15/30% protein.  **Mismatch vs subject:** mostly normal weight; carbohydrate not restricted.
- **Finding / number:** 30% protein lowered intake (7.21 vs 9.33-9.62 MJ/d in 2013; 8.73 vs 9.30-9.48 in 2014). There was **no leverage in the low-protein direction**. That is the opposite asymmetry to Gosby 2011, and "complete protein leverage did not occur."
- **Quality / limitations:** 12-day averages, with no reported day-to-day oscillation.

### 4. FGF21 — the protein-status hormone points the other way
- **Citation:** Laeger T et al., 2014, "FGF21 is an endocrine signal of protein restriction," *J Clin Invest* 124:3913-3922, PMID 25133427. Hill CM et al., 2019, "FGF21 signals protein status to the brain and adaptively regulates food choice and metabolism," *Cell Rep*, DOI 10.1016/j.celrep.2019.05.022, PMID 31167139. Hill & Morrison 2019, *Obesity*, DOI 10.1002/oby.22568, PMID 31339002. Nicolaisen TS et al., 2025, *Nat Metab* 7:602-616, PMID 40050437.
- **Population:** mostly rodents; humans in the 28-day low-protein arm (Laeger) and in lean men over 5 weeks (Nicolaisen).  **Mismatch vs subject:** complete. These are protein-*restriction* models.
- **Finding / number:** FGF21 rises with **low** protein (hepatic Fgf21 up within 24 h; ~10-fold higher circulating levels in rodents) and drives protein-seeking. None of these studies shows an FGF21 or other protein-status signal causing a discrete low-intake day after protein excess.
- **Quality / limitations:** strong mechanistic work, but it does not address the subject's question.

**Supporting / conflicting:**
- Johnstone AM et al. 2008, *AJCN* 87(1):44-55, PMID 18175736: 17 obese men; high-protein ketogenic vs high-protein moderate-carb, 7.25 vs 7.95 MJ/d. BMI not confirmed.
- Veldhorst MA et al. 2010, *Br J Nutr* 104(9):1395-1405: carbohydrate absence and β-hydroxybutyrate contribute to high-protein appetite suppression.
- Gibson AA et al. 2015, *Obes Rev* 16(1):64-76, PMID 25402637, DOI 10.1111/obr.12230: ketogenic low-carb diets gave less hunger and lower desire to eat. The changes were small, and their main benefit was preventing the rise in appetite that energy restriction usually brings.
- Leidy HJ et al. 2015, *AJCN* 101(Suppl):1320S-9S.
- Levitsky DA et al. 2017, *Appetite* 116:277-283.
- Tarasuk V & Beaton GH 1991, *AJCN* 54:464-470, PMID 1877501. Beltsville study, n=29; up to 37% of within-person variance was patterned (for example weekly), not random.
- Dietitian II Study 2013, PMID 24021734: healthy women, CV ~25%.
- Johnstone AM, Stubbs RJ, Harbron CG 1996, *Eur J Clin Nutr* 50:418-430, PMID 8862477: one day of overfeeding with high-protein, high-carbohydrate or high-fat food. A secondary source reports that high protein gave more satiety but **no greater next-day intake reduction**. That detail is **UNVERIFIED** from the paper itself.
- Lim JJ et al. 2022, PMC8838013 (journal not confirmed): 121 women with obesity (BMI 35.4). The planned protein and carbohydrate contrasts were not achieved in practice, and intake was similar between groups.

## Disagreements between sources
- **Which direction protein leverage works in.** Gosby 2011 found leverage on the low side only, with no effect from 15% to 25%. Martens 2013/2014 found it on the high side only, at 30% versus 5%, with no low-side effect. The 2019 theory paper claims both directions. Protocols differed (4 vs 12 days, menu design, protein source), and none of the trials was in obese or insulin-resistant people.
- **Whether intake compensates from day to day.** de Castro's classic work describes 2-3-day lagged compensation. Secondary sources say other groups placed the lag at 3-5 days. Levitsky 2017 found no negative autocorrelation at all and attributes the earlier findings to a statistical artifact. I could not fetch de Castro's papers, so **the specific lags are UNVERIFIED**.
- **How much of the high-protein effect is ketosis.** Weigle shows protein alone is enough. Johnstone 2008 and Veldhorst 2010 show ketosis adds to it. The two effects are additive, and in the subject they cannot be separated.

## Applying it to the subject
- **Mean and SD of his daily intake.** At ~2050 kcal/day and a CV of ~23-25%, one within-person SD is **~470-510 kcal**. A "very low" day of ~1300-1500 kcal is only about 1-1.5 SD below his mean. Under normal variation, roughly one day in 6-7 falls more than 1 SD low, which is about once a week, before counting 12-hour workdays and skipped meals.
- **Why "after 2-3 high-protein days" is not a real test.** He eats ~150-180 g protein almost every day. So *every* low day follows 2-3 high-protein days by default. The pattern has no contrast condition, which makes it textbook selection or overfitting.
- **Which confounds are live.** Mild ketosis (carbohydrate ~30-60 g, lower still on a missed-meal day) is one. Workload and meal skipping are another. Regression to the mean after higher-intake days is a third.
- **What would test it.** Log intake daily for 4-6 weeks, including protein grams, carbohydrate grams, and a workday-intensity flag. Then check whether a low-intake day is predicted by protein from the previous 2-3 days *after* adjusting for workload. A cheap optional extra is urine ketone strips.
- **What is reasonable to believe.** His ~30-35%-of-energy protein share plus low carbohydrate is probably suppressing his *average* intake by a few hundred kcal/day. That is consistent with Weigle, Martens and Johnstone [likely]. A specific lagged "protein rebound low day" is not supported [likely].

Confidence: **medium**. The component findings are well established and consistent at abstract level: protein satiety, the PLH's proportion-based claim, and FGF21 as a low-protein signal. But no study directly tests multi-day carryover, the trial populations do not match the subject, and I could verify sources only from search-index abstracts, not full texts.

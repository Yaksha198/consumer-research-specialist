## Plain-language answer
The fasting measures (fasting insulin, and HOMA-IR, which mostly reflects the liver) improve quickly once someone is in a calorie deficit. The liver responds within 48 hours to 7 days, before much weight is gone. After that, the improvement tracks how much weight is lost. The best per-kg figure I found is about **0.15 HOMA-IR units per kg lost** (roughly 6.5 kg per 1 point). A large 8-week trial found about **−1.4 units after ~11% weight loss**. For someone starting at 3.58, that means a fall of about 30–40% after 6–10 kg of loss, giving a true value of roughly **2.0–2.7** at 12 weeks. A very-low-carb diet probably pushes fasting insulin a bit lower than weight loss alone would. However, HOMA-IR varies about 27% within the same person from week to week. A single retest could therefore read anywhere from about 1.5 to 3.2 even if the improvement is real. Only a drop of more than about 47–52% counts as proven change from one pair of measurements.

## Insulin resistance / population caveat
- **Most of the rapid-reversal data come from people with type 2 diabetes** (Lim, Petersen, DiRECT, Goldenberg). Their hepatic insulin resistance and liver fat start much higher, so they improve more in absolute terms. The subject's HOMA-IR of 3.58 is insulin-resistant but his glycaemic status is unknown. Prediabetes data (PREVIEW, Cell Metab 2026) and obese non-diabetic data (Magkos, EJCN) match him better. [likely]
- **HOMA-IR mostly measures hepatic, fasting insulin resistance.** It does not capture muscle insulin sensitivity. Muscle is where the lack of resistance training matters: in Beals 2023, diet plus exercise gave about 2× the whole-body (mainly muscle) insulin-sensitivity gain of diet alone at matched 10% loss. So skipping training probably costs less on HOMA-IR than on clamp or OGTT measures. [likely]
- **Very-low-carb diets affect the two inputs in different ways.** They lower fasting insulin partly through carbohydrate restriction itself, not only weight loss (Kirk; Cell Metab 2026). They may also slightly raise fasting glucose through reduced glucose uptake in muscle, or "glucose sparing". In Bisschop 2001, a eucaloric high-fat, low-carb diet in healthy men reduced insulin's ability to suppress liver glucose output. Because HOMA-IR = glucose (mmol/L) × insulin (µU/mL) / 22.5, a lower insulin and a higher glucose can partly cancel out. The effect on glucose is supported mechanistically. I found no verified size for it in obese insulin-resistant adults. [guessing on size]
- **Active energy deficit versus weight-stable state:** Kirk showed that 48 h of restriction improves hepatic insulin sensitivity before meaningful weight loss. A retest taken during an active deficit therefore includes a "deficit effect" that may partly fade at maintenance. [likely]
- **Population mismatch:** most participants were middle-aged (44–50), and many trials were mostly women. The subject is a 32-year-old man. Lim 2011 was 9/11 male. [certain]

## Sources

### 1. Body-composition MRI regression (EJCN)
- **Citation:** Authors not captured (UNVERIFIED). Impact of weight loss-associated changes in detailed body composition as assessed by whole-body MRI on plasma insulin levels and homeostatis model assessment index. *Eur J Clin Nutr* 2016/17. PMID 27759067; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27759067/ (search-snippet/abstract only)
- **Population:** n=50, age 20–69, BMI 21.6–54.4; low-calorie diet or bariatric surgery; mean loss −16.0±13.6 kg. **Mismatch vs subject:** includes surgical patients and very large losses; sex split not seen.
- **Finding / number:** 6.5 kg lost per 1-point fall in baseline-adjusted HOMA; 2.9 kg per 1 µU/mL fasting insulin. Liver-fat change explained 55.1% of the variance in HOMA change.
- **Quality / limitations:** small and mixed-intervention; a linear fit across very different losses; not a meta-analysis.

### 2. PREVIEW 8-week low-energy diet
- **Citation:** Christensen P, et al. 2018. Men and women respond differently to rapid weight loss: metabolic outcomes of a multi-centre intervention study after a low-energy diet in 2500 overweight, individuals with pre-diabetes (PREVIEW). *Diabetes Obes Metab.* DOI 10.1111/dom.13466; PMID 30088336; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30088336/ (abstract only)
- **Population:** n=2,224 (1,504 women, 720 men), overweight with prediabetes; 90.8% completed. **Mismatch vs subject:** used meal replacements (~800 kcal/d, a detail from memory and UNVERIFIED), not a low-carb diet eaten ad lib at ~2,000 kcal; baseline HOMA-IR not captured.
- **Finding / number:** weight loss 11.8% in men and 10.3% in women; HOMA-IR fell 1.50±0.15 in men and 1.35±0.15 in women; 35% returned to normal glucose.
- **Quality / limitations:** very large, but single-arm with no control; a faster, deeper deficit than the subject's.

### 3. Magkos 2016 dose-response
- **Citation:** Magkos F, Fraterrigo G, Yoshino J, et al. 2016. Effects of moderate and subsequent progressive weight loss on metabolic function and adipose tissue biology in humans with obesity. *Cell Metab* 23(4):591–601. DOI 10.1016/j.cmet.2016.02.005; PMID 26916363 (abstract only)
- **Population:** 40 sedentary men and women, 44±12 y, BMI 37.9±4.3, insulin-resistant by HOMA and clamp. **Mismatch vs subject:** older; not low-carb.
- **Finding / number:** weight loss of 5.1% (n=19), 10.8% (n=9) or 16.4% (n=9) vs maintenance (n=14). A 5% loss improved liver, adipose and muscle insulin sensitivity and β-cell function. Further loss mainly added muscle insulin sensitivity. The claim that liver and adipose sensitivity plateau at ~5–8% loss surfaced in search text and may come from the authors' discussion or a later Klein review [likely]. I did not capture per-tissue % changes.
- **Quality / limitations:** gold-standard clamp with tracers; very small higher-loss arms.

### 4. EuBIVAS biological variation
- **Citation:** The biological variation of insulin resistance markers: data from the European Biological Variation Study (EuBIVAS). *Clin Chem Lab Med* 2024. DOI 10.1515/cclm-2024-0672 (abstract only; authors not captured)
- **Population:** 90 non-diabetic adults (52 women, 38 men) from 5 countries, 10 weekly fasting samples. **Mismatch vs subject:** mostly not insulin-resistant; variation is probably larger in insulin-resistant people (Jayagopal 2002).
- **Finding / number:** within-person variation of HOMA-IR 26.7% (95% CI 25.5–28.3), driven mainly by insulin; QUICKI 4.1%.
- **Quality / limitations:** strong design for this purpose; covers biological variation only, not differences between labs.

## Disagreements between sources
- **Linear per-kg vs early plateau.** The EJCN regression treats HOMA change as linear in kg lost. Magkos suggests liver and adipose gains are mostly done by ~5–8% loss. That would put most of the subject's HOMA-IR benefit in the first ~5–9 kg, with less per kg afterwards. I have not resolved this; I treat 0.15/kg as a rough figure for the first ~10 kg only. [likely]
- **Low-carb advantage.** Kirk (48 h) and Cell Metab 2026 (at 10% loss) show a clear hepatic and insulin advantage for very-low-carb diets. Goldenberg's meta-analysis in type 2 diabetes found no significant insulin-sensitivity difference, and benefits faded by 12 months. The short-term fasting-insulin advantage looks real, but its lasting size is unclear. [likely]
- **Fasting glucose on low-carb.** Very-low-carb diets lowered 24-h glucose by 20% vs 8% (Cell Metab 2026). The "physiological insulin resistance" idea predicts slightly higher morning glucose, but the evidence for it in obese insulin-resistant adults is mechanistic and partly from blogs. [guessing]

**The amount of weight lost is uncertain.** He reports 9 kg. BMI going from 35 to 33 at typical height implies about 6 kg (on ~105–110 kg, roughly 5.5–8.5%). The table works through both, plus a possible 12 kg by week 12.

- **Honest headline:** expect about 3.58 → **2.0–2.7**, roughly a 25–45% fall. Most of the hepatic part should already be in place in the first 2–5 weeks. [likely] A very-low-carb diet may push fasting insulin lower than this, but a small rise in fasting glucose could offset part of it. [guessing]
- **What the retest can and cannot prove:** with ~27% variation, a single pair of results proves real change only if the retest is ≤ ~1.7–1.9, which is a fall of more than ~47–52%. A reading of 2.3–2.8 is **consistent with improvement but not proof of it**. It is not a failure. [likely]
- **Retest protocol:**
  - Same lab and same insulin assay (assays can differ by around 24%).
  - Same fasting length (10–12 h), same time of morning, no hard exercise the day before.
  - Same diet state as the baseline test, with no carb refeed or cheat day in the preceding 2–3 days.
  - Ideally 2–3 draws, or at least fasting insulin and glucose reported separately, so a change in glucose alone can be seen.
  - Note whether the baseline test was taken on his old higher-carb diet. If so, part of any drop reflects the change in diet, not only improved insulin sensitivity.
  - A test taken during an active deficit will look better than one taken at maintenance.

Confidence: **low–medium**. The direction and speed (days to weeks, front-loaded) are well supported. The per-kg size rests on one small regression and one uncontrolled trial. Within-person variation (~27%) is about as large as the effect being measured. The amount he has actually lost is uncertain.
